## How it got here

Microsoft built its fortune selling Windows and Office to nearly every company on earth, then spent the 2010s turning those customers into cloud subscribers through Azure and Microsoft 365. Under Chief Executive Satya Nadella, who took over in 2014, it rebuilt itself around recurring subscription revenue.

Its early bet on OpenAI, from 2019, put it at the front of selling generative AI to companies, and the price of that lead has been spending. Microsoft paid $39.4 billion in cash for data centers and equipment in the quarter to September, more than double the $19.4 billion a year earlier, its filings show.

Last week the spending story turned outward. On Friday, Bloomberg reported that Microsoft is switching off a 2023 deal to rent AI capacity from Oracle ($ORCL) as part of a review of its outside cloud contracts, Reuters carried the report, and CNBC reported it is still deciding which specialist AI renters to keep ([our brief](/news/2026-11-06/microsoft-msft-is-winding-down-a-deal-to-rent-ai-capacity-from-oracle-orcl/)). **Is Microsoft spending its way into a weaker business, or buying its next decade of growth?**

## The business in one line

Companies and households pay Microsoft every month or year for software subscriptions, cloud computing and AI tools, and a smaller share buys Windows, Xbox and Surface devices.

## The numbers that matter

### Growth: 18.8% last quarter, faster than its five-year pace

Revenue grew **18.8%** from a year earlier in the quarter to September, faster than its five-year pace of 14.6% a year, though slightly below the 20.5% median of the 32 software companies we cover. For a company with $346 billion of sales over the last 12 months, that adds about a mid-sized software company every quarter.

### Profitability: a 47.1% operating margin

Microsoft kept **47.1%** of its sales as operating profit over the last 12 months, in the top tenth of its peers, whose median is 9.7%, and 5.2 points more than five years ago. Its gross margin is the weak spot: 68.2% over two years, in the bottom quarter of software peers, because renting out computers costs more than selling software licences.

### Cash: free cash flow of 17.3% of sales

Free cash flow, the cash left after paying for data centers and equipment, was **17.3%** of sales over the last 12 months, in the bottom quarter of its peers, against 21.5% over two years. In dollars it fell to $67.0 billion in the fiscal year to June 2026 from $74.1 billion two years earlier, while sales rose 35%.

### Returns: about 26.5% on capital

Return on capital was about **26.5%**, in the top quarter of its peers, against a median of 9.7%, and return on equity 30.3%. Those returns are what the spending has to protect: if new data centers earn less, this number falls first.

### Balance sheet: $15.2 billion of net debt

Microsoft ended September with $20.3 billion of cash against $35.5 billion of debt, our fundamentals data show: **$15.2 billion** of net debt, about five weeks of operating profit. Unlike Oracle, which sold $20 billion of new shares last week, Microsoft is paying for its build-out from operations.

## Why the stock is where it is

1. **The spending is eating the cash.** Capital spending in the September quarter was more than double a year earlier, and free cash flow fell to $18.6 billion from $25.7 billion.
2. **One customer looms large in the backlog.** At the end of December 2025, about 45% of Microsoft's $625 billion of signed commercial contracts were tied to OpenAI, Microsoft said at the time, The Register and Fierce Network reported.
3. **The AI trade broke last week.** Microsoft **fell 3.6%** on 5 November in the midterm sell-off, and the next day's Oracle report raised the question of whether it is buying less outside capacity.

## Testing the worry

**The cash squeeze is temporary, so far.** Operating cash flow rose to $58.0 billion in the September quarter from $45.1 billion a year earlier, up 29%, so the spending is being paid for by a growing business, not borrowed. Chief Financial Officer Amy Hood said spending was below forecasts because "some third-party capacity deliveries we expected in Q1 were delayed," according to a transcript from TradingKey, which means more of the bill is still to come.

**The OpenAI exposure is contained.** In the December 2025 quarter, the 55% of the backlog not tied to OpenAI grew 28%, Fierce Network and TechRadar reported. The total reached $677 billion at the end of September, up 73%, the results release showed. A weaker OpenAI would slow Microsoft's growth; it would not empty the order book.

**The supplier review spreads, but outward.** Winding down rented capacity hurts the renters more than Microsoft: CoreWeave ($CRWV) fell 49.4% between 6 October and Friday while Microsoft rose 1.5%, in our data. Microsoft has committed more than $80 billion to outside providers, CNBC reported, so trimming them is a choice to own more of its capacity, which costs more up front and keeps the margin in-house.

The worry that survives is the first one, in a milder form: free cash flow will stay low for as long as Microsoft keeps raising its spending.

## The moat

1. **Its customers are already signed in.** The Microsoft Cloud, which bundles Azure, Office and other online services, brought in $63.9 billion in the September quarter, up 30%, the release showed: selling AI to companies that already run Microsoft 365 needs no new sales relationship.
2. **Scale in cloud that only two rivals match.** Azure's $33.2 billion grew 48%, against Amazon's ($AMZN) AWS at $45.98 billion and 39% growth ([our recap](/news/2026-10-29/amazon-amzn-q3-aws-speeds-up-but-ads-and-profit-miss-estimates/)) and Alphabet's ($GOOGL) Google Cloud at $30.71 billion and 103% growth ([our story](/news/2026-10-29/alphabet-googl-shares-plunge-after-search-falls-short-and-cash-burn-grows/)).
3. **Contracts signed years ahead.** The $677 billion of remaining obligations is about two years of current sales already booked.

## The risks we'd watch

- **Capital spending outrunning cash.** If spending stayed at the September quarter's pace for a year, it would take about $157 billion, four-fifths of the $196 billion of operating cash flow of the last 12 months.
- **OpenAI.** At the December 2025 share, OpenAI's commitments were worth about $281 billion; a delay in OpenAI's own fundraising would show up there first.
- **The gross margin.** It slipped to 67.8% in the September quarter from 69.0% a year earlier, in our data; each point is worth about $3.5 billion a year of gross profit on current sales.

## Our verdict

**Business: Strong. Price: Below its usual price.** Microsoft grows faster than it did five years ago, keeps nearly half its sales as operating profit and pays for the AI build-out out of its own pocket. The P/E of 27.7 sits below its two-year median of 32.9, which is cheap on earnings but not on cash. What would change our mind is operating cash flow growing more slowly than capital spending for a full year; the December-quarter report, due in late January, is the next check. Microsoft has never earned more, and it has rarely kept less of it as cash.
