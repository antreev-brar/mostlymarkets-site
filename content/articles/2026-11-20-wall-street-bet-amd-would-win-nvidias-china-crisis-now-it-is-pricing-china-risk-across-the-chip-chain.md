A week ago the story was that Nvidia's ($NVDA) China scandal would hand its customers to AMD ($AMD) and Broadcom ($AVGO). This week investors started telling a darker one: the China problem is not Nvidia's alone, and anyone in the AI chip chain with ties to Chinese buyers will be made to answer for them. Hunterbrook Media put it most bluntly on Thursday, when it disclosed a short position in AMD alongside an investigation of its own.

The peg is a run of five days. A US rule on chip rentals took effect, TSMC ($TSM) and Samsung halted some shipments to China, California subpoenaed Nvidia, Hunterbrook went after AMD, and Alibaba ($BABA) was sued by its own shareholders. AMD has **lost 16.4%** since its 12 November close, the day Jensen Huang resigned and the day it peaked on the "winner" story, by our data. It was down another 6.2% before Friday's open.

## The story in one sentence

> "Investors treated $AMD as the winner of Nvidia's China crisis. The problem: AMD has a China problem of its own." — Hunterbrook Media, in a post on X on 19 November announcing its investigation, as CNBC and Yahoo Finance reported

The chain runs like this. If Megaspeed, a Singapore firm, really moved restricted Nvidia chips to Chinese companies, then regulators, prosecutors and plaintiffs will look at every chipmaker, contract manufacturer and cloud customer that sold to or through firms like it. Each new inquiry costs money and time, can freeze sales while licences are sought, and gives short sellers a template. The stocks with the most China business get marked down first, and the ones selling to US cloud giants get marked up.

## The evidence for it

### 1. Washington's rule now reaches the factories

The Commerce Department issued a rule on 16 November requiring licences to send advanced chips to overseas data centers that serve Chinese companies, CNBC and Nextgov reported, and it took effect on Thursday. That same day the Financial Times reported that **TSMC and Samsung** had suspended some shipments to Chinese customers while they seek licences, as Reuters and Newsquawk relayed ([our story](/news/2026-11-19/tsmc-tsm-and-samsung-halt-some-chip-shipments-to-chinese-customers-under-new-us-curbs/)). The curbs began as a fix for one loophole; within three days they were holding up the contract chipmaker's orders.

### 2. The investigations have multiplied

The Justice Department now leads a criminal investigation of Megaspeed that Commerce opened in 2024, the two agencies said on 17 November, as The Hill and the South China Morning Post reported. The SEC is examining Nvidia's disclosures, the Wall Street Journal reported on 17 November, and California Attorney General Rob Bonta served Nvidia a subpoena on Thursday over possible chip diversion and what investors were told, CNBC and CBS News reported ([our story](/news/2026-11-19/california-subpoenas-nvidia-nvda-over-china-chips-and-what-investors-were-told/)). Supermicro ($SMCI) has been named in a reported Justice Department smuggling inquiry ([our story](/news/2026-11-13/supermicro-smci-shares-plunge-on-a-report-of-a-doj-smuggling-inquiry/)).

### 3. Short sellers are hunting China links, and AMD has more of them

Muddy Waters disclosed a short on Nvidia on 17 November; Hunterbrook followed on AMD two days later, alleging that IEIT Systems, a Chinese server maker on the US blacklist since March 2025, kept getting newer AMD firmware ([our story](/news/2026-11-19/amd-amd-shares-plunge-after-hunterbrook-shorts-it-over-china-links/)). The exposure is not small: customers billed in China and Hong Kong paid AMD about **$7.75 billion**, or 22.4% of its 2025 revenue, according to its annual report as Hunterbrook and Blofin cited it. That counts where customers are billed, not where chips end up.

### 4. The lawsuits have reached the customers

Alibaba shareholders filed a proposed class action in Manhattan on Thursday, saying the company hid its ties to Megaspeed, Reuters and mlq.ai reported ([our brief](/news/2026-11-20/alibaba-baba-is-sued-by-shareholders-over-its-ties-to-megaspeed/)). Megaspeed helped move at least **$7.5 billion** of Nvidia chips to Chinese companies, The Information reported, as Fortune and the Daily Signal relayed. ## The case against it

The strongest objection is that most of this week's selling has nothing to do with China. On Thursday 531 of the 580 stocks we track fell and the S&P 500 lost 2.0% for a sixth straight day ([our Daily](/daily/2026-11-20/)), and Intel ($INTC), which has no part in the scandal, is down 9.0% since 12 November. AMD rose 30.3% in a week on the "winner" story, so some of its fall is that bet unwinding rather than a new China discount.

1. **The market is unwinding AI, not pricing China.** TSMC, which [halted shipments](/news/2026-11-19/tsmc-tsm-and-samsung-halt-some-chip-shipments-to-chinese-customers-under-new-us-curbs/) to comply, was up 1.2% before Thursday's open and fell later with everything else. This is the strongest counter-point: the stocks fell together, so the China story explains the order, not the size.
2. **Nobody has been charged.** Hunterbrook said its findings do not establish a breach of export law, and AMD denied wrongdoing, Reuters and Yahoo Finance reported. Renting chips to Chinese firms through overseas data centers was not banned under the old rules, the Daily Signal and The Substrate noted; the Atlantic Council argued the case shows controls that work, only not well enough.
3. **Nvidia's numbers already exclude China.** Its $130 billion fourth-quarter guide assumes no China data center compute revenue, the release said, and it still topped the $125.1 billion analysts expected, according to LSEG data cited by CNBC ([our recap](/news/2026-11-17/nvidia-nvda-tops-q3-estimates-and-guides-to-130-billion-in-its-first-report-without-huang/)). Its board said it has no reason to believe secrets were compromised.

## What would change minds

- **Huang and Nvidia General Counsel Tim Teter sit for closed-door interviews with three House committees on 1 and 2 December**, Axios and UPI reported. A referral or new names from those sessions would widen the story; a quiet session would cool it.
- **How fast Commerce grants TSMC's and Samsung's licences.** Approvals within weeks would make the halt a paperwork delay; a long freeze would turn a legal risk into lost sales.
- **Any US inquiry into AMD's IEIT firmware.** So far only a short seller has raised it. If Commerce or the Justice Department opens one, the AMD discount would have an official basis; if nothing comes by AMD's next report, the stock's fall is the place this story is most overdone.

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The legal web is real and spreading: in five days it moved from one company to a contract manufacturer, a rival and a customer, and each step came from an agency, a court filing or a named short seller. The weakest link is the jump from inquiry to lost sales, since Nvidia has already taken China out of its numbers and no chipmaker has been charged. The number we'll watch is AMD's gap to Broadcom: since 6 November it has opened to about nine points, and the House interviews on 1 and 2 December are the first event that can widen or close it.
