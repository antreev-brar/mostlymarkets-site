The story Wall Street is telling about AI this month is that the neoclouds, the specialist companies that borrow to fill data centers with Nvidia chips and rent them to the tech giants, are where the buildout breaks first. Fund manager Dan Niles put it most bluntly on CNBC this week: the neoclouds carry leveraged balance sheets and heavy negative cash flow, and when the cycle turns, most of them could "go to zero," Benzinga and KuCoin News reported.

The news peg came on Friday, when CNBC and Bloomberg reported that Microsoft ($MSFT), the neoclouds' biggest customer, is narrowing the list of outside providers it rents from and winding down a 2023 deal with Oracle ($ORCL). The stakes, from our price files: CoreWeave ($CRWV) **fell 49.4%** between 6 October and Friday's close, and Nebius ($NBIS) 48.2%, while Microsoft was flat. CoreWeave reports on Tuesday, 10 November.

- **Why it matters:** The neoclouds borrowed tens of billions against contracts with a handful of buyers. If those buyers rent less, the debt stays and the revenue doesn't.

## The story in one sentence

> "If they're borrowing at 9% and the best return they can get off of that is a 5%, they're destroying value at every turn." (Gil Luria, D.A. Davidson analyst, on CoreWeave, in mid-October, as reported by MarketScreener and Crypto Briefing)

The chain runs like this: neoclouds borrow at high rates to buy chips and build halls for a few big customers; if those customers delay or trim their rentals, revenue arrives later than the interest bills; lenders then demand more, which squeezes the next round of building, which is what the bond market has started to price.

## The evidence for it

### 1. Microsoft spent $5.5 billion less than expected on capacity
Microsoft's capital spending and finance leases came to **$45.2 billion** in its September quarter, below the $50.7 billion analysts expected according to StreetAccount, CNBC and Seeking Alpha reported on 28 October. Chief Financial Officer Amy Hood said some third-party capacity deliveries "were delayed." CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN fell the next day.

### 2. Microsoft is trimming the roster of outside renters
On Friday, CNBC reported that Microsoft plans to keep a few key outside suppliers and lean more on its own data centers, and Bloomberg reported it is winding down the 2023 Oracle rental deal ([our brief](/news/2026-11-06/microsoft-msft-is-winding-down-a-deal-to-rent-ai-capacity-from-oracle-orcl/)). A Microsoft spokesperson called the review "a normal and responsible way to manage large, complex infrastructure projects," CNBC and RuntimeWire reported. It has not said which providers it will drop.

### 3. It has already passed on one expansion and tightened oversight on others
Microsoft declined an option to more than double its contract with Nscale at a Texas site, the Financial Times reported, and Bloomberg reported on Thursday that it has tightened oversight of projects tied to Nebius, IREN and Nscale and is stepping away from sites it deems risky. Nebius and IREN fell 14% and 17% that day, Reuters reported.

### 4. Lenders are charging more than ever
The yield on CoreWeave's 9.625% bond due 2032 rose above **16%** on Friday, CNBC reported, from about 15% earlier in the week, Yahoo Finance reported. Against that coupon, a yield of 16% means investors now price real odds of loss on a company that carries more than $50 billion of debt.

## The case against it

Goldman Sachs analyst Gabriela Borges upgraded CoreWeave to Buy on 19 October, arguing that investors overestimate its future capital costs and underestimate its returns, CNBC and 24/7 Wall St. reported; 31 of 45 analysts rate it a buy, according to LSEG data cited by CNBC.

1. **The orders are still growing.** CoreWeave's backlog **rose 246%** in a year to $104 billion, not counting more than $25 billion of new commitments signed early in the third quarter, the company said in August, and it raised its 2026 revenue forecast to $12.4 billion to $13.2 billion.
2. **Microsoft says the shortfall is timing.** Hood guided capital spending in the December quarter to more than $50 billion, CNBC and Seeking Alpha reported. Delayed deliveries are a construction problem, not a sign the giants want less computing.
3. **The pullback is narrower than the selloff.** A person familiar told Reuters the Nscale decision was made months ago and its existing contract is unaffected, and Bloomberg's oversight report named no cut to a Nebius contract.

The strongest counter-point is the first: a backlog that keeps growing is hard to square with a demand collapse. The weakest link in the bull case is that backlog is only worth what it costs to fund, and that cost has just jumped.

## What would change minds

- **CoreWeave, Tuesday 10 November after the close:** a backlog above $104 billion and an unchanged $12.4 billion to $13.2 billion revenue forecast would undercut the story; any change to a Microsoft contract, or a cut to its capital spending plan for lack of funding, would confirm it.
- **Nebius's third-quarter report, expected next week:** whether its Microsoft capacity is being delivered and paid for on schedule.
- **Microsoft's December-quarter spending, due in late January:** above $50 billion, as Hood guided, with outside leases intact, would say the giants still need the renters.

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The financing strain is well documented: a bond yielding over 16%, a Microsoft spending miss blamed on late outside capacity, and a named review of the renters it uses. The weakest link is demand: CoreWeave's backlog more than tripled in a year and Microsoft guided its spending higher, so what the evidence shows today is costlier money and slower delivery, not customers walking away. The one number we'll watch is CoreWeave's backlog on Tuesday, set against how much more it will have to borrow to deliver it.
