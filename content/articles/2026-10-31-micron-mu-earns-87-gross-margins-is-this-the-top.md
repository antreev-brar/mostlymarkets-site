## How it got here

Micron ($MU), founded in Boise, Idaho, in 1978, is one of three companies that make most of the world's DRAM, the working memory in every computer, alongside Samsung and SK hynix. It also makes NAND flash, the chips that store data. Memory is close to a commodity, so the business swings with supply: when chips run short, prices and profits jump, and when the makers' new factories arrive, both collapse.

Its last two booms ended that way: profits peaked in fiscal 2018 and again in fiscal 2022, and within a year or two Micron was earning a fraction as much, or losing money. Then AI changed what memory is worth: Nvidia's processors need stacks of high-bandwidth memory (HBM), and making it crowds out the supply of ordinary DRAM.

On 30 September Micron reported fiscal 2026 revenue of **$133.2 billion**, and Chairman and Chief Executive Sanjay Mehrotra said he expects "an even stronger fiscal 2027." New multi-year customer contracts, he said, "provide added confidence in the durability of Micron's financial performance." The shares fell 16.9% in October anyway.

**Are 87% gross margins Micron's new normal, or the top of another cycle?**

## The business in one line

Cloud companies, server makers, and phone and PC makers buy Micron's memory and storage chips, now partly under multi-year take-or-pay contracts that fix volumes and set price bands, rather than one quarter at a time.

## How it makes money

DRAM brought in **$100.7 billion** of fiscal 2026 revenue, about three-quarters, and NAND flash $31.8 billion, the annual report shows. Micron sorts its customers into four units:

Revenue has risen in all but one of the last 11 quarters, and Micron expects about **$61.5 billion** this quarter, its guidance shows.

## The numbers that matter

### Growth: more than tripled, now slowing fast

Revenue grew 74% quarter on quarter in the three months to May; this quarter's guide implies **about 13%**, measured against a fourth quarter that had an extra week.

Micron's growth comes in lurches: sales fell in three of the last nine fiscal years, by half in fiscal 2023. This is cyclical growth, not compounding.

### Profitability: the highest operating margin in tech

Micron's operating margin was **74.6%** in fiscal 2026, the highest of the 91 information-technology companies whose operating margins we track, and above the 65.2% Nvidia ($NVDA) earned over its last 12 months.

The swing comes from costs that barely move with prices: in fiscal 2026 Micron's cost of goods sold rose 14% while revenue more than tripled. The same leverage works in reverse; in fiscal 2023 the operating loss came to 37.0% of sales.

### Cash: $59.0 billion of free cash flow in a year

Free cash flow, the cash left after paying for factories and equipment, was **$59.0 billion** in fiscal 2026. That's more than three times what Micron produced in the previous nine years combined, by our sum of its SEC figures.

Little of it went to shareholders: buybacks and dividends came to about $1.3 billion, while Micron repaid $10.0 billion of debt and kept the rest. For a business that lost money three years ago, we think that's the right order.

### Balance sheet: about $68 billion of net cash

Micron ended the fiscal year with **$73.5 billion** of cash and investments against $5.2 billion of debt, its results release shows.

Customers supplied $12.75 billion of that cash as deposits under the new contracts, nearly all due back between 2029 and 2031, the annual report says. Net of those, Micron holds about $56 billion, while its plans put fiscal 2027 capital spending above $50 billion.

## Why the stock is where it is

1. **The cycle looks late.** The shares set their closing high on 25 June, the day after Micron's third-quarter report, and were 22.7% lower by 7 July, when Yahoo Finance declared a bear market in memory stocks. The last cycle turned the same way: the shares hit their 2022 closing high on 14 January, and revenue rose for two more quarters before falling by more than half. Pzena Investment Management had argued in May that memory margins are "likely to mark the peak of the current cycle."
2. **China is building.** CNBC reported on 15 October that a new chip design from China's CXMT and fears of a glut in 2029 were hitting memory stocks. Micron fell 19.2% from 7 October to 16 October, by our price files.
3. **The price increases are shrinking.** TrendForce expects conventional DRAM contract prices to rise 10% to 15% this quarter, after an estimated 93% to 98% in the first quarter ([our chart](/news/2026-10-31/memory-chip-prices-are-still-rising-just-more-slowly/)). Micron's own guide calls for a slightly lower gross margin than last quarter's.
4. **Its customers are strained.** Alphabet and Meta burned cash last quarter, and on 29 October Intel blamed memory shortages for weak PC demand. Micron fell 5.3% the next day as chip stocks slid.

## Testing the worry

**The late cycle: lasting, but probably shallower.** Most of Micron's new take-or-pay contracts set prices within minimum and maximum bands, and Micron expects even their floor prices to yield gross margins "meaningfully above our peak quarterly margins in any past cycle." In the 2018 boom, its quarterly gross margin topped out at **61.0%**. Counterpoint Research's Jeongku Choi told CNBC on 15 October that such deals mean "the down cycle will differ from previous ones."

But the floor covers only part of the business. The annual report values the contracts' committed volumes at minimum prices at about **$134 billion** over five years, and only about a quarter of that is due in the next 12 months, roughly half of what Micron expects to sell this quarter alone. We weigh how much of 2027 is already booked in [our story on AI memory](/news/2026-10-31/ai-memory-for-2027-is-sold-out-before-its-made-what-the-evidence-says/).

**China: real, but later.** Kyung Kye-hyun, a former head of Samsung's chip division, said in May that Chinese output could push prices down from late 2027 or early 2028, Notebookcheck reported. Rosenblatt Securities' Kevin Cassidy told CNBC that CXMT's new design won't "swing the supply-demand equation at all," and Quilter Cheviot's Ben Barringer questioned its yields.

**Shrinking increases: not yet a fall.** Prices are still rising, and part of the slowdown is the contracts themselves: TrendForce says long-term deals will make some suppliers' server-memory increases lag the market. The same bands that cap the rises limit the falls.

**Strained customers: spreading in PCs and phones, contained in data centers so far.** Intel's warning and a 29% quarter-on-quarter drop in Sandisk's ($SNDK) consumer sales, which its management tied to higher prices, show costlier memory is cutting into consumer demand. That hits Micron where its margins are richest: its phone-and-PC unit earned an 88% operating margin last quarter, the highest of its four units.

Data-center buyers are still spending: Amazon expects capital spending of about $220 billion this year.

The worry that survives is the first: the contracts may soften the next downturn, but they don't prevent one, and Counterpoint expects DRAM prices to fall about 23% in 2028, CNBC reported.

## The moat

Micron's moat is membership of a three-company club, not dominance within it.

1. **Scale in a three-way market.** Samsung, SK hynix and Micron took 87.6% of DRAM revenue in the second quarter, TrendForce estimates.
2. **HBM know-how.** Micron began volume shipments of HBM4 for Nvidia's Vera Rubin platform in the first quarter of calendar 2026, it said in March; Samsung and SK hynix make it too.
3. **Binding contracts.** Buyers who miss their minimum purchases can forfeit all or part of their deposits, and $7 billion of letters of credit back some of the agreements, the annual report says.

| Company | Share of DRAM sales, April to June 2026 | Operating margin, latest quarter |
|---|---|---|
| Samsung | 39.4% | 72.4% (chip division, to 30 September) |
| SK hynix | 24.9% | 79.7% (to 30 September) |
| Micron | 23.3% | 80.7% (to 3 September) |

The club keeps newcomers out of HBM for now. It didn't stop Micron's 2023 slump.

## The risks we'd watch

1. **A price reset in 2028.** If Micron's prices fell by Counterpoint's 23% from this quarter's guide, with volumes and costs unchanged, its operating profit would drop by roughly 28%, by our arithmetic; a 2023-style halving of revenue would cut far deeper.
2. **A few big buyers.** About half of revenue came from its ten largest customers in each of the last three years, the annual report says. If its data-center buyers, about 61% of sales, cut their purchases by a fifth, Micron would lose about 12% of revenue.
3. **Its own building boom.** Micron plans to spend about $25 billion on plant and equipment, net of government incentives, in the first half of this fiscal year, nearly as much as in all of fiscal 2026, and more in the second half. Its annual report warns that the contracts could feed the next glut: makers are lining up supply to match them, which would be too much if buyers broke their commitments and demand fell short, and buyers taking more than they need to meet their minimums could build up inventories.

## What the price assumes

We compare Micron with Sandisk, Western Digital ($WDC), the hard-drive maker, and Nvidia, using Friday's close, SEC diluted earnings and CNBC's quote data.

| Measure | Now | Its history | Peers |
|---|---|---|---|
| P/E, last 12 months | 11.9x | 21.2x median since October 2021, on days with trailing profits; low of 5.6x in 2022 | Sandisk 9.2x, Western Digital 14.2x, Nvidia 25.9x |
| Forward P/E, next 12 months | 5.0x | Not in our data | Sandisk 5.4x, Western Digital 14.9x, Nvidia 16.9x |
| Price to free cash flow | 17.0x | Not meaningful: free cash flow was negative or near zero in fiscal 2023-25 | Sandisk 9.6x, Western Digital 32.0x, Nvidia 38.8x |

The median S&P 500 stock in our data trades at 23.5 times trailing and 19.1 times forward earnings.

At **5.0 times** expected earnings, the price assumes Micron's profits shrink sharply after the next year. Before this month, the last time its trailing P/E was this low was March 2023, three months before its 12-month earnings turned into a loss.

At its usual multiple, today's price would match annual earnings of about **$42 a share**, by our arithmetic, little more than the roughly $38 Micron expects to earn in this quarter alone. That's still more than it earned in any year before fiscal 2026: the price assumes a deep downturn, not a return to the old cycle's profits.

## Our verdict

**Business: Solid. Price: Below its usual price.** Micron earns the highest operating margin in tech in our data, holds about $68 billion of net cash, and sells part of its output under take-or-pay contracts with price floors. We call the business solid rather than strong because it lost money as recently as fiscal 2023, and those contracts have never been tested in a downturn.

At 11.9 times trailing earnings the stock is cheap on paper, but cheap is what memory stocks look like at the top, and the contracts cover too little of its sales to make today's margins the new normal. What would change our mind: a contract backlog that keeps growing would make the price look too gloomy, while an adjusted gross margin below the 86.25% Micron guided when it reports in December would say the turn is coming early.

A business at its best moment, priced as if the moment won't last.
