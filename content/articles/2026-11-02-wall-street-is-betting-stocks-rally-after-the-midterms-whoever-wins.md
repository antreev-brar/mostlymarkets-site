In an interview published by Bloomberg on Sunday, Morgan Stanley's chief US equity strategist, Mike Wilson, said that investors should treat any dip around Tuesday's midterm elections as a chance to buy, whoever wins. He leans on one of the market's best-known records: the S&P 500 has been higher six and 12 months after every midterm since 1950. That is the story Wall Street is telling going into the vote: the election clears uncertainty, and stocks rally after it.

The news peg is the odds. Prediction markets now give Democrats a 72% chance of winning both the House and the Senate, Reuters reported on Monday; on 27 October, Kalshi's odds of a sweep stood at 63%, CNBC reported. AI stocks led the pre-election slide: Nvidia ($NVDA) **fell 11.4%** in the month to Friday, while Centene ($CNC), a Medicaid insurer JPMorgan expects to benefit if Congress changes hands, rose 12.2%.

## The story in one sentence

> "What I consistently hear from investors is that the opposition to data centers is really about the political backlash and that after the midterms things get a lot better." — Ed Mills, Washington policy analyst at Raymond James, to CNBC on 9 October

The chain investors draw: the vote removes a known unknown, so the volatility priced in before it fades; President Donald Trump keeps his veto whoever controls Congress, so no sweeping law gets through; and stocks then trade on earnings again. If that holds, the past month's sell-off in AI stocks is a dip, not a turn.

## The evidence for it

### 1. The S&P 500 has never been lower a year after a midterm

Since 1950 the index has been higher 12 months after all 19 midterm elections, according to Morgan Stanley data cited by Bloomberg and an analysis by 24/7 Wall St.; Deutsche Bank found the same 19-for-19 record from 1946 to 2018, Reuters reported in 2022. The average gain varies with the start date and method, from about 15% to 21%. Midterm years themselves tend to be weak, with an average drop of about 17% from peak to trough within the year, according to an analysis of data since 1950 by Dreyer Wealth.

### 2. Even a sweep leaves a divided government

A Democratic Congress would face Trump's veto on any bill that hurts the tech industry, Bloomberg and Reuters both noted last week. Gridlock has been the better result for stocks: JPMorgan strategist Dubravko Lakos-Bujas found a 21% gain over the two years after elections that produced divided government, against 18% after single-party control, measured from election day, CNBC and Yahoo Finance reported on 5 October.

### 3. Options traders are pricing a routine event

Man Group said options markets treat the vote as "relatively routine" with a clear outcome, CNBC reported on 29 October. The VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, stood at **19.15** early Monday, well below its 52-week high of 35.30.

### 4. The sell-off has already priced some of it

Wilson said a few "clearing events" (the midterms, stress over AI spending, and tighter liquidity) could pull the S&P 500 about 9% below its record, to roughly 7,100, before it rises to 8,000, Bloomberg and Yahoo Finance reported. The index closed Friday at 7,580.07, 3.4% below its 52-week high of 7,844.52.

## The case against it

Bank of America's chief investment strategist, Michael Hartnett, warned that a Democratic sweep could drive a sell-off of more than 10% that would hit AI stocks, Invezz and Crypto Briefing reported in October, and BofA moved to "risk-off" for the month, CNBC reported on 16 October. Three counter-points:

1. **AI policy risk starts after the vote, not before it.** Mills told CNBC that the view that things improve after the midterms is wrong: "after the midterms, things get worse." Democrats are expected to push for tighter AI oversight, and Evercore ISI warned that AI hearings could hit Nvidia and Amazon ($AMZN), CNBC reported on Monday. Morgan Stanley's Ariana Salvatore said the vote matters most for laying the groundwork for data-center legislation in 2029, Bloomberg reported. This is the strongest point: hearings, subpoenas, and state fights over power prices do not need 60 Senate votes.
2. **A slow count would hit positions built for a clean one.** Man Group warned a delayed result could catch markets off guard, CNBC reported. In 2020, control of the Senate was not decided until Georgia's runoffs in January.
3. **A small sample.** Nineteen elections is a short record, and none of the usual explanations for it (relief from uncertainty, a weak run-up) speaks to a war with Iran or oil near $100 a barrel.

## What would change minds

- **Election night, Tuesday 3 November.** A clean Democratic sweep called by Wednesday is the priced case; a Senate count that drags on is not.
- **AMD reports after the close on Tuesday,** the first AI test after the vote; a weak data-center outlook alongside a sweep would make the AI sell-off look like more than a dip.
- **The jobs report on Friday 6 November.** With the 10-year yield at 4.93%, a hot number could matter more than the vote.

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The post-midterm record is real and long, and the veto means even a sweep cannot pass laws that hurt AI spending, so a fall in the S&P 500 on Wednesday would not on its own break the story. The weak link is the assumption that the risk clears on election night: for AI and data centers, a Democratic Congress brings hearings and state-level fights that start in January, and AI stocks carry this market. The number we'll watch is the Senate call: a sweep settled by Wednesday morning is priced; a contested Senate is not.
