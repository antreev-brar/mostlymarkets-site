The story investors are telling this month is that technology no longer leads the US stock market: money is leaving AI and chip stocks for drugmakers, grocers and household brands, and the rest of the market can hold up without them. Bank of America strategist Michael Hartnett put it plainly in a note on Tuesday 17 November: "Euphoria on boom & AI over."

His firm's survey, taken from 6 to 12 November, found the share of fund managers overweight technology fell to a net **12% from 37%** in October, the biggest one-month cut since 2006, CNBC and Fortune reported. In our data, the average S&P 500 stock is up 1.8% since Nvidia's ($NVDA) closing high on 6 October, while the index's combined market value is down 7.2%.

## The story in one sentence

> "Euphoria on boom & AI over." (Michael Hartnett, chief investment strategist at Bank of America, in a note on 17 November 2026)

The chain runs like this: if investors stop paying up for AI spending, the biggest companies in the index stop driving its gains; if earnings outside tech keep growing, money moves to those companies instead; and the S&P 500, a third of which is tech, stalls even as the typical stock does fine. That is a rotation, not a crash, and it lasts only as long as tech's earnings disappoint and everyone else's don't.

## The evidence for it

### 1. The S&P 500's tech stocks lost 17.6% in six weeks

Weighted by size, the S&P 500's information technology stocks have lost **17.6%** of their market value since 6 October, by our calculation from Wednesday's closes. Over the same weeks, consumer staples rose 13.0%, health care 8.6% and consumer discretionary 5.5%.

The worst of it came on Thursday 5 November, when the S&P 500's tech sector fell 7.3%, its biggest one-day drop in more than six years, and the S&P 500 lost 4.4%, Reuters and StockAnalysis reported. Investors feared Democratic midterm wins would slow data-center building.

### 2. Fund managers made their fastest exit from tech since 2006

Bank of America's November survey of fund managers found a net 12% overweight in tech, down from **37%** a month earlier, CNBC, Fortune and Benzinga reported. Hartnett said the survey still "does not show enough investor 'fear' to buy", Stocktwits and Fortune reported, so his team stays cautious.

### 3. The other 493 companies are growing earnings faster

In the third quarter, the seven biggest tech companies grew earnings **22.6%** from a year earlier, against 32.6% for the other 493 companies in the S&P 500, FactSet's John Butters wrote on 3 November. The seven had missed estimates by 14.7% in aggregate at that point, while the whole index beat by 1.9%. That count came before Nvidia reported.

### 4. Even a big Nvidia beat didn't stop the slide

On Tuesday, Nvidia said its revenue rose 103% to $115.8 billion and guided to $130 billion for this quarter, above forecasts, [as we reported](/news/2026-11-17/nvidia-nvda-tops-q3-estimates-and-guides-to-130-billion-in-its-first-report-without-huang/). The stock rose after hours, then closed Wednesday **down 2.9%** at $150.96, 36.9% below its 6 October high.

## The case against it

Morgan Stanley's Mike Wilson is the loudest doubter. On 16 November he moved global stocks to "maximum overweight" and raised his S&P 500 target to **8,700** from 8,000, about 15% above the index's 13 November close, citing earnings growth, lower oil and continued AI spending, StockAnalysis and Stocktwits reported. Three points weigh against the rotation story:

1. **The index can't rise without tech.** Even after the fall, tech is about a third of the S&P 500's value by our count, and Reuters noted on 9 November that it still sets the market's direction. The S&P 500's equal-weighted gain this year is 11.7% against 6.0% weighted by size, by our calculation, so the broad market has done better, but it has not lifted the index.
2. **Tech's earnings are still growing fast.** Nvidia's revenue doubled in a year, and its forecast beat estimates. A rotation built on earnings needs tech's earnings to slow, and the latest report says they haven't.
3. **Tech is now the cheap side.** Nvidia trades at about **12.5 times** expected earnings in our data, [below its chipmaker peers](/news/2026-11-18/2-stocks-we-like-after-their-earnings-and-1-wed-skip-nvidia-disney-and-tyson-foods/). Costco trades at 48 times, Walmart at 43 and Eli Lilly at 34.

The third is the strongest: rotations end when the price gap gets too wide, and the defensive stocks now carry the high multiples.

## What would change minds

- **Anthropic's first trade, today, Thursday 19 November.** It priced at $83 a share, above its $70 to $78 range, CNBC and TechCrunch reported. A strong debut would show money still wants AI; a weak one would feed the rotation.
- **Dell reports after the close on Tuesday 24 November**, and Salesforce and Snowflake on Wednesday 2 December. Any cut to AI server or software forecasts would confirm the earnings case against tech.
- **The Fed decides on Wednesday 9 December.** The minutes of its October meeting show almost all officials expect more hikes, and some flagged AI as an inflation risk, CNBC and Investing.com reported. Higher rates weigh hardest on stocks priced for distant growth.

Hartnett's own test is the next survey: in his reading, the best times to buy tech came after fund managers went underweight, and at a net 12% overweight they haven't yet.

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The rotation is real in the numbers: the average stock beat the index by nine points in six weeks, fund managers made their fastest tech exit in 20 years, and earnings outside the biggest tech names grew faster last quarter. The weakest link is the price: the money has gone to staples and drugmakers that now cost more than Nvidia per dollar of expected profit, and Nvidia's revenue just doubled. The number we'll watch is Dell's AI server forecast on 24 November.
