The story Wall Street is telling this week is that the AI boom runs on a loop of money, and that the loop will break first where it is most borrowed. Kristalina Georgieva, managing director of the IMF, told CNBC on Monday that deals in which money invested in an AI company comes back to the investor as orders for chips or computing now total **a little over $1 trillion**, about two-thirds of it funded with debt, CNBC and NewsBytes reported.

The peg is the loop's biggest lender losing its dealmaker. Nvidia ($NVDA), which invests in and guarantees the customers that buy its chips, lost Chief Executive Jensen Huang on 12 November, and The Wall Street Journal reported on Sunday that interim Chief Executive Colette Kress is expected to rein in that financing, The Next Web and Guru3D reported. Since Wednesday's close, the last before Huang quit, CoreWeave ($CRWV) has **fallen 12.6%** and Nebius ($NBIS) 25.1%, while Microsoft ($MSFT) and Amazon ($AMZN) are flat, in our price data.

This is the next chapter of the story we laid out on 7 November, [that the neoclouds are AI's weak link](/news/2026-11-07/wall-street-is-betting-the-neoclouds-are-ais-weak-link-coreweaves-report-will-test-it/). CoreWeave's report on 10 November answered the demand question. What changed since is the supply of money.

## The story in one sentence

> The global economy is "standing on one leg, on the AI leg." Kristalina Georgieva, IMF managing director, to CNBC at the ASEAN summit in Manila on 16 November.

The chain runs like this: AI labs and cloud renters sign huge contracts for chips and computing; their suppliers, Nvidia above all, help pay for those contracts with equity stakes, guarantees and promises to rent back spare capacity; lenders then lend against those promises. If the supplier steps back, lenders ask for more, the borrowers with the thinnest cash flow pay more for money or build less, and the orders that flow back to the chipmakers shrink.

## The evidence for it

### 1. The IMF says the loop has topped $1 trillion

Georgieva put AI circular financing at just over $1 trillion on Monday, with **about two-thirds** borrowed, and said it is "not a huge size, but it is a rapid increase," CNBC and MLQ reported. The IMF's October Global Financial Stability Report already named a correction in AI stocks among its three top near-term risks, and said investors in these deals had secured $1.4 trillion of future revenue commitments, CNBC and Quartz reported on 13 October.

### 2. Nvidia is the loop's biggest private lender

Bank of America estimated in August that Nvidia had committed about **$70 billion** to equity stakes in AI companies and about $230 billion to guarantees and backstops, Reuters and TheStreet reported. Its July quarterly filing listed $36 billion of cloud agreements with neoclouds, in which Nvidia agrees to rent back capacity it has sold, Reuters and CNBC reported. Guarantees are not cash out the door, Benzinga noted, but they are what lets a small cloud company borrow against a rack of chips.

### 3. Its new boss is expected to lend less

Kress, Nvidia's finance chief since 2013, is expected to scale back financing for AI start-ups and neoclouds, the Journal reported on Sunday, and Huang had already told neocloud partners in his final months to get their own customers to pay upfront, The Next Web and Guru3D reported. Reuters reported on Monday that the $500 billion platform Nvidia set up with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR in August will likely survive, but that lenders want future deals backed by customer contracts or guarantees.

Part of Nebius's fall is its own: it trimmed its 2026 outlook on 12 November ([our story](/news/2026-11-13/nebius-nbis-q3-sales-jump-571-but-it-trims-its-2026-outlook-and-plunges-17/)). In the bond market, CoreWeave's 2032 notes yielded **more than 16%** on 6 November, CNBC and Yahoo Finance reported, a price lenders charge to a company they think could struggle to refinance.

Of the roughly $90 billion Anthropic plans to raise this week, $26 billion comes from Nvidia, Google, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC, companies it buys chips and cloud capacity from or partners with ([our narrative](/news/2026-11-15/wall-street-is-betting-anthropics-ipo-can-rescue-the-ai-trade-3-numbers-will-decide-it/)). ## The case against it

The strongest doubter is the person who raised the alarm. Georgieva called the total "not a huge size" and said recent market moves had been orderly and were not undermining the global economy, CNBC and MLQ reported. Morgan Stanley's strategists, led by Michael Wilson, went further on Sunday, moving global stocks to "max overweight" because they judge the AI spending cycle intact, Bloomberg and MarketWatch reported.

1. **The demand behind the loans is real.** CoreWeave reported a contracted backlog of about **$132.5 billion** on 10 November and raised its 2026 revenue forecast to $13.0 billion to $13.3 billion, its release showed. A lender with a signed customer contract behind a loan is lending against that customer, not against Nvidia's goodwill.
2. **The biggest borrowers can still borrow at will.** Meta ($META) drew a record €53 billion of orders for its debut euro bond, up to €9 billion, on 4 November, Bloomberg and NewsBytes reported. The companies that pay for most of the build-out have deep markets open to them, so a pullback by Nvidia narrows the loop at the edges, not at the center.
3. **No lender has walked away yet.** The Journal's report is about what Kress is expected to do; Reuters reported no sign from Nvidia of a pause, and the August platform with six of the world's largest money managers is still discussing deals.

The strongest is the second. If the money moves from small renters to Microsoft, Amazon and Meta rather than leaving the AI build-out, chip orders hold even as CoreWeave and Nebius pay more for funding.

## What would change minds

- **Tuesday, 17 November, after the close: Nvidia's report.** It guided to $108 billion of third-quarter revenue. The number that matters for this story is in its quarterly filing: the size of its guarantees and cloud agreements against the $36 billion of July, and whether Kress says anything on the 5 p.m. ET call about the financing program.
- **Wednesday 18 and Thursday 19 November: Anthropic's pricing and first trade.** A strong debut gives the loop an exit to public money; a weak one leaves suppliers like Nvidia holding more of it.
- **Wednesday, 18 November: the Fed's minutes.** With the 10-year yield at 5.13% and futures pricing a December hike, the cost of the debt in the loop rises with every hawkish line, and the most indebted renters feel it first.

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The size of the loop is documented by the IMF, Nvidia's role in it by its own filing, and the market is already pricing the break where the story says it would: at the renters that borrow against Nvidia's backing, not at the giants that pay their own way. The weakest link is the claim that the money is leaving. So far it is a report of what Kress is expected to do, set against record demand for Meta's bonds. The number we'll watch is the guarantee and cloud-agreement total in Nvidia's next quarterly filing: below $36 billion in cloud agreements would be the first hard sign the loop is shrinking.
