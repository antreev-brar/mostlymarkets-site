Will the Federal Reserve raise rates again on 9 December while tech stocks are in a correction? The question reopened at 8:30 a.m. ET on Friday, when employers added **165,000 jobs** in October against the 65,000 economists expected, and futures traders pushed the odds of a quarter-point hike to about 78%, up from about 67% on Thursday, Reuters reported ([our jobs story](/news/2026-11-06/employers-added-165-000-jobs-in-october-keeping-a-december-hike-in-play/)).

The other number in the argument is the stock market. The Nasdaq Composite closed Friday at 24,579.43, **11.3% below** its 52-week high, after its worst day since April 2025 on Thursday, in our data. The Fed's benchmark rate sits at 3.75% to 4% after September's hike, its first since July 2023.

## The case for a hike

### Mary Daly, president, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco

**Higher rates are likely to be needed, and the sell-off won't stop them.** On Friday afternoon, hours after the jobs data, Daly said further increases "are likely to be needed" to bring inflation down, though how much and when is undecided, CNBC and Bloomberg reported. She called the tech-stock slide "painful" but orderly and said she would prefer to move gradually, Reuters reported ([our brief](/news/2026-11-07/feds-daly-says-more-rate-hikes-are-likely-needed-despite-a-painful-tech-sell-off/)).

### Mark Zandi, chief economist, Moody's Analytics

**Inflation plus strong hiring gives the Fed room to move.** Fed officials are "still very nervous about the high rates of inflation," Zandi told CNBC on Friday, and with this jobs number "they've got latitude to raise rates," Benzinga also reported. He is not a full hawk on the economy: he said "a lot of noise" in the report overstates the labor market's strength.

### Preston Caldwell, economist, Morningstar

**The report removed the Fed's main reason to wait.** "The Fed will be more eager to hike rates in December after today's jobs report," Caldwell wrote on Friday. Revisions added 84,000 jobs to August and September, and hourly pay rose **0.4%** in October, above forecasts, Morningstar and CNN reported, the faster wage growth the Fed worries can feed inflation.

## The case against

### Lee Hardman, senior currency analyst, MUFG

**Falling stocks are already doing the Fed's work.** The tightening of US financial conditions "could prove more important in deciding Fed policy action in December" than Friday's jobs report, Hardman said, Reuters reported via Investing.com and Pound Sterling Live. When share prices fall, companies pay more to raise money and households feel poorer, which slows demand without the Fed moving.

### Tom Lee, head of research, Fundstrat Global Advisors

**September's hike already looks like a mistake.** Lee called the post-midterm AI sell-off an "overreaction" on CNBC last week, and argued that recent productivity data make September's increase look like an error, so the Fed may not hike again in December, Benzinga and Bloomingbit reported. Faster productivity lets wages rise without pushing up prices, which undercuts the wage half of the hawks' case.

## Where they agree

Both sides accept that the stock slide has tightened conditions; they disagree on whether it is enough. Even Ariane Curtis of Capital Economics, who expects a hike, wrote that equity weakness "is contributing to tightness in financial conditions," but doubted "those moves have been enough so far to rule out a December rate hike," Pound Sterling Live reported. And nobody on either side argues inflation is back at the Fed's 2% goal: consumer prices rose 3.7% in the year to September.

The market has picked a side in one corner. On Friday, as the Nasdaq rose 1.1%, the stocks most exposed to higher borrowing costs fell.

## The number that decides it

October's consumer price index, at 8:30 a.m. ET on Tuesday, 10 November. Economists in Bloomberg's survey expect prices to have risen **0.3%** in the month after September's 0.6%, with core prices, which leave out food and energy, up 0.2%, Business Standard and Morningstar reported. The report will carry no 12-month rate, because October 2025 prices were never collected during that year's shutdown. After that, the November jobs report on 4 December lands four days before the Fed meets.

## Our read

The hawks have the stronger evidence today: hiring sped up, pay rose faster than forecast, and a Fed president said after the sell-off that it won't hold her back. The doubters' best point is one they share with us: the inflation that justified September's hike came from oil above $100, and US crude closed Friday at **$90.34**, so energy should pull October's CPI down ([our narrative on the hike](/news/2026-11-06/why-wall-street-thinks-the-fed-will-hike-into-a-stock-sell-off/)). If Tuesday's core reading comes in at 0.2% or less, we think 78% odds are too high; at 0.4% or more, the debate is over.
