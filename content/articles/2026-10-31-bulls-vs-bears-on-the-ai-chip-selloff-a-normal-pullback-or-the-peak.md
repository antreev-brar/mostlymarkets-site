The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index **sank 7.2%** on Friday, NBC News and Reuters reported, after Intel ($INTC) blamed a memory-chip shortage for a weak forecast and cash burn at Amazon ($AMZN) revived worries about the cost of AI. The Nasdaq Composite had its biggest one-day drop since 5 June, Reuters reported.

At the center of the argument is next year's AI budget: Goldman Sachs expects the five biggest US hyperscalers, the companies that run giant data centers, to put about **$1.2 trillion** into AI infrastructure in 2027, up from about $800 billion this year.

## The bull case

### Keith Lerner, chief investment officer, Truist

**Tech's drop is a normal pullback in a bull market.** Lerner set the slide against the run before it: semiconductors have risen more than 380% since the bull market began in October 2022, and tech's decline "does not appear abnormal relative to prior pullbacks during this bull market," he wrote in a note quoted on Yahoo Finance's live blog on Friday. He isn't calling the bottom: Truist sees **roughly 2% to 8%** more downside for tech.

### David Goeckeler, chief executive, Sandisk

**Memory demand will grow in PCs, phones and data centers next year.** Asked about PCs on Thursday's earnings call, the evening Intel warned about them, Goeckeler said demand for the flash memory Sandisk ($SNDK) makes will grow next year in "PCs, smartphones, data center," because each device keeps getting more of it. Sandisk's data-center revenue **rose 61%** from the previous quarter, but the stock still plunged 13.5% on Friday.

### Ryan Hammond's team, Goldman Sachs

**AI spending will rise by about half next year.** The team's $1.2 trillion forecast for 2027 is above the roughly $1.1 trillion Wall Street expected, Bloomberg reported on 25 September. The spenders have since backed the direction: Alphabet ($GOOGL) Chief Financial Officer Anat Ashkenazi said on its 28 October call that she expects capital spending "to increase significantly in 2027," and Amazon Chief Executive Andy Jassy said on Thursday's call that Amazon expects to "spend meaningfully more in 2027."

## The bear case

### Jay Goldberg, analyst, Seaport Research Partners

**Intel's collapse is a planning failure in the worst PC market in 15 years.** Intel **plunged 28.6%** on Friday, its worst day since the 1970s, CNBC reported ([our breakdown](/news/2026-10-31/intel-intc-lost-28-6-in-a-day-is-the-business-broken/)), and Goldberg told Reuters the drop reflects "a loss of faith in management's credibility." In his view Intel is making too many PC chips and too few for AI servers, in what he called the biggest growth cycle ever for chips: a bear case on Intel and PCs, not on AI demand.

### Lee Min-hee, analyst, BNK Investment & Securities

**The pace of AI building may already have peaked.** Lee told the Korea JoongAng Daily on 29 October that the pace of AI infrastructure investment may have peaked in the third quarter, and that big tech companies "will keep facing weaker free cash flow and funding pressure." The companies' filings back her on cash: from July to September, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta ($META) and Microsoft ($MSFT) together spent **$1.15** on property and equipment for every dollar their operations brought in, against 61 cents a year earlier, by our count ([our story on the cash scorecard](/news/2026-10-31/free-cash-flow-is-now-the-scorecard-for-ai-spenders-what-the-evidence-says/)).

### Ludovic Subran, chief investment officer, Allianz

**The debt behind AI data centers was priced too tight for the risk.** Subran told the Financial Times in mid-October that "data center bonds are having their Darwin moment, where the weakest credits struggle to survive," according to EnterpriseAM's summary of the FT story. The same summary said one CoreWeave ($CRWV) unsecured bond had fallen below 80 cents on the dollar, pushing its yield toward 15%.

## Where they agree

Neither of Friday's triggers was a drop in AI orders. Amazon's was the cost of its build-out, and Intel's was PCs: memory makers have shifted production toward high-bandwidth memory for AI systems, sending memory prices soaring, Reuters reported. On Thursday's call, Intel Chief Executive Lip-Bu Tan said customers had begun signaling a PC slowdown in recent weeks "based on the memory prices and availability."

## The number that decides it

The number is next year's AI spending: how fast it grows, and how much of it the spenders can pay for from their own cash. Chipmakers' AI sales come out of that budget and rise and fall with it, and even Goldman's forecast has the budget growing 54% next year but only 12% in 2028.

The first read comes on Tuesday 3 November, when AMD ($AMD) reports after the close, with a call at 5 p.m. ET; its forecast for the December quarter will show whether AI orders are still growing ([our preview](/news/2026-10-29/amd-amd-reports-earnings-on-tuesday-what-to-watch/)). Arm ($ARM) and Qualcomm ($QCOM) report on Wednesday 4 November, and both depend on phones, where pricier memory is squeezing phone makers. Nvidia ($NVDA), the biggest test, reports on Tuesday 17 November.

## Our read

**The evidence favors the bulls on demand and the bears on cash.** Nvidia, the biggest maker of AI chips, fell least of the 12 stocks in our chart, which fits a scare over PCs and costs rather than over orders ([where the damage landed](/news/2026-10-31/the-chip-index-fell-7-2-in-a-day-where-did-the-damage-land/)). But Alphabet, Amazon and Meta already invest more than their operations bring in, so 2027's budgets will lean harder on balance sheets and debt markets, where Subran sees the cracks.

The normal-pullback view also undersells the damage in chips, where the slide began long before Friday. Nine of the 12 stocks set their highs between late May and the end of June and sit 21% to 53% below them; only Nvidia, TSMC ($TSM) and AMD peaked with the Nasdaq in October.
