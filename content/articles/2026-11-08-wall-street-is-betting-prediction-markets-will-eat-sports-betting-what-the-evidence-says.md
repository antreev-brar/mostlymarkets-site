The story Wall Street is telling about gambling stocks this fall is that prediction markets, the federally regulated exchanges where customers trade yes-or-no contracts on games, are taking the sports bettor away from the sportsbooks. Bank of America analyst Shaun Kelley said it plainly at the NEXTPredict Summit on 23 October: Wall Street had underestimated how fast prediction markets would grow and how hard they would compete, Bloomingbit and CDC Gaming reported.

The news peg came on Thursday evening, when DraftKings ($DKNG) said spending on its own Predictions product would cut this year's adjusted profit by nearly three-quarters. The stakes, from our price files: DraftKings **fell 21.3%** on Friday to $18.51, its biggest one-day drop since November 2022, and is down 46.3% this year, while Robinhood ($HOOD), which earns more from event contracts than from crypto trading, rose 3.2%.

## The story in one sentence

> Prediction markets are growing faster, and competing harder, than Wall Street expected. (Shaun Kelley, Bank of America analyst, at the NEXTPredict Summit on 23 October, as reported by Bloomingbit and CDC Gaming)

The chain runs like this: an exchange regulated by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission can offer sports contracts nationwide to anyone 18 or older, including in states where sports betting is illegal; that lets it grow faster than state-licensed sportsbooks; the sportsbooks must then build their own exchanges and pay for customers, which costs them profit before it earns them anything.

## The evidence for it

### 1. Kalshi's value nearly doubled in five months
Kalshi raised $1 billion at a **$40 billion** valuation on 29 October, up from $22 billion in May, CNBC and iGaming Chronicle reported. Sports are most of its business: trackers cited by iGaming Chronicle put sports at 84% of its notional volume in the 30 days to 6 October, and the company puts the share at around 90%, Yahoo Finance reported.

### 2. Robinhood's event contracts now outearn its crypto trading
Robinhood earned **$173 million** from event contracts in the third quarter, up from $104 million in the first, its results release and 10-Q filings show, and traded a record 16.4 billion contracts, more than seven times a year earlier. Its 10-Q says the trading was concentrated in sports. Crypto revenue fell 55% from a year earlier, Yahoo Finance reported, so event contracts passed it.

### 3. The sportsbooks are paying to catch up
DraftKings plans $650 million to $700 million of Predictions investment this year and $250 million to $350 million next year, PYMNTS and Gaming America reported, and cut its 2026 adjusted EBITDA forecast to **$175 million to $275 million** from $700 million to $900 million, its release said. Flutter, owner of FanDuel, said on 4 November that its Predictions spending will continue into 2027 at similar levels, Legal Sports Report and SBC Americas reported. Chief Executive Dan Taylor admitted FanDuel's product was "not competitive" at first.

### 4. The leader is still far ahead
DraftKings says its exchange was the third-largest in the US in October with about 3.6% of notional volume, but a measure of dollars actually risked from Aldrin Research puts it fifth, behind Kalshi, Polymarket US, Novig and OG, Sportico reported. FanDuel Predicts handled $119 million of volume in the third quarter, Flutter's release said, a sliver of the tens of billions of dollars trackers count on Kalshi each month.

## The case against it

The doubters are the courts, and some of the analysts who know the industry best. Citizens analyst Jordan Bender has said prediction markets will last but hit ceilings from regulation, elections and the leagues' own involvement, and his team's June note put DraftKings' own prediction business at about $1 billion of revenue by 2030, as complementary revenue rather than a replacement, SBC Americas and SCCG reported.

1. **Two appeals courts say states can regulate the contracts.** The Sixth Circuit ruled in late September that Ohio and Tennessee can apply their gambling laws to Kalshi's sports contracts, following a Ninth Circuit ruling for Nevada, CNBC and CoinDesk reported. Only the Third Circuit, in April, sided with Kalshi, and New Jersey has asked the Supreme Court to overturn it, Paul Weiss and Holland & Knight wrote.
2. **The losses are piling up.** On Friday, 6 November, a federal judge in San Francisco barred Kalshi from offering sports contracts on the lands of the California tribes suing it, the first injunction a tribe has won against it, SBC Americas reported. If the 50-state reach shrinks to the states that already license sportsbooks, the exchanges' main edge goes with it.
3. **The sportsbooks are keeping their revenue so far.** DraftKings' third-quarter revenue still rose 16% from a year earlier, to $1.33 billion, its release said, and Flutter's guidance cut was blamed mostly on sports results and Brazil, not on lost customers, its release said.

The strongest counter-point is the first. The bull case for prediction markets rests on federal law overriding state gambling rules, and the appeals courts are now 2 to 1 against that reading.

## What would change minds

- **The Supreme Court's decision on New Jersey's petition:** if it takes the case, the 50-state model stays in doubt until a ruling, probably in 2027; if it declines, the split between the circuits stands and states keep pressing their cases.
- **February's Super Bowl:** the biggest betting day of the year, and the first since the exchanges' growth spurt; state regulators' handle reports for February would show whether sportsbooks are losing bets to the exchanges.
- **DraftKings' fourth-quarter results, early next year:** the first check on whether Predictions is on the way to the $650 million to $750 million of 2027 revenue it is guiding to as its spending falls ([our DraftKings story](/news/2026-11-06/draftkings-dkng-sinks-27-as-predictions-spending-guts-its-profit-outlook/)).

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The shift of money is in the filings: Kalshi's value nearly doubled in five months, Robinhood's event-contract revenue grew about sixfold in a year, and DraftKings is now spending most of a year's profit to compete. The weakest link is the law: the exchanges' edge is reaching bettors in every state, and two of three appeals courts have said states can regulate them. Robinhood, which earns event-contract revenue without a sportsbook to defend, has still fallen 28.5% this year, which tells us the market is pricing the legal risk as well as the growth. The number we'll watch is Robinhood's fourth-quarter event-contract revenue in February: growth through a football season with state cases mounting would say the courts haven't capped it yet.
