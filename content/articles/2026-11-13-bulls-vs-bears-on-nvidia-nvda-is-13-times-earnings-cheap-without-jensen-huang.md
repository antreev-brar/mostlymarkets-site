Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's ($NVDA) chief executive on Thursday 12 November, with finance chief Colette Kress named interim CEO, the company's filing shows. Since then Wall Street has split on one question: is a company still growing revenue at 106% a year now cheap, or is the discount the right price for losing the man who built it?

The number at the center is the valuation. At $160.33 at 10:30 a.m. ET on Friday, Nvidia traded at **13.2 times** forward earnings, against a median of 22.2 times for the 25 chipmakers we track, in our data. Its own trailing multiple has a median of 45.9 times since August 2024.

## The bull case

### Jordan Klein, technology trading strategist, Mizuho
**Nvidia is "silly cheap" at under 10 times earnings on his estimates.** Klein told CNBC on 10 November, two days before the resignation, that the stock trades at less than 10 times expected earnings on his estimates, unusually low for a company whose profits are still growing, investingLive and TipRanks reported. He granted that investors see Huang as irreplaceable, and that the open-ended timetable was the market's biggest worry. Mizuho still cut its target to $250 from $315.

### Brian Colello, equity strategist, Morningstar
**The AI build-out shows no sign of slowing, so the earnings haven't changed.** Colello said Morningstar was "stunned and disappointed" by the reports about Huang but left its financial forecasts and its $310 fair value unchanged, Yahoo Finance reported. "We still have little evidence of a pause in the AI buildout," he said this week, as Investing.com reported.

### Stifel's semiconductor team
**Keep buying, but at a lower multiple.** Stifel kept its buy rating and cut its target to $205 from $315, Investing.com and FourWeekMBA reported. The whole cut is in the multiple: it now pays 13 times its fiscal 2028 earnings estimate of $15.65 a share, down from 20 times, and treats a leadership transition as its base case.

## The bear case

### Stacy Rasgon, semiconductor analyst, Bernstein
**The problem is leadership, and more bad news may come.** Rasgon cut Nvidia to market perform from outperform on 12 November and halved his target to $200 from $400, investingLive and GuruFocus reported. He kept his earnings forecasts, saying AI demand is still very strong, but warned against buying the dip while further allegations and the choice of successor are unresolved, Bloomingbit reported.

### Rick Schafer, semiconductor analyst, Oppenheimer
**Losing Huang is a "material, potentially thesis-changing event."** Oppenheimer cut the stock to perform from outperform and withdrew its price target, Investing.com and TokenPost reported. The firm said there is no obvious successor and warned of a brain drain if senior engineers follow Huang out.

### Dan Ives, technology analyst
**Huang himself is worth a quarter of the company.** Ives estimated that Nvidia's value would be 25% to 30% lower without Huang, The Wealth Advisor and BigGo reported. On Nvidia's $3.86 trillion market value at 10:30 a.m. Friday, that's roughly $1 trillion pinned to one person.

## Where they agree
Every one of these analysts, bulls and bears, left the demand picture alone: none cut revenue or earnings forecasts because of the resignation. The argument is entirely about the multiple, which is why price targets fell across the board even at firms that kept buy ratings.

## The number that decides it
The next figure is Nvidia's own outlook. It reports third-quarter results on Tuesday 17 November, its first report without Huang. If Kress guides revenue up again and the big cloud buyers repeat their spending plans, the bulls' "nothing has changed" holds; a softer forecast, or any sign of senior departures, gives the bears their proof.

## Our read
The bears are right that the multiple should come down: a company run by its founder for three decades carries real key-person risk, and Stifel's move from 20 to 13 times is an honest way to price it. But at 13.2 times forward earnings, Nvidia already trades below the chipmaker median while growing faster than 86% of them, so the market has priced in more than a leadership change; it has priced in a business that slows. **Too early to tell** whether that happens, and Tuesday's outlook is the first number that can settle it.
