The story Wall Street started telling on Thursday is simple: the Democrats who won this week's midterms will slow the building of AI data centers, and everyone who sells into those buildings will sell less. Orion Chief Investment Officer Tim Holland told CNBC, as Quartz reported, that investors were stepping back until they understand what the results mean for AI capital spending. Reuters said fears of tougher AI rules gave the Nasdaq its biggest one-day drop in 19 months.

The news peg is Texas. On Tuesday, Democrat Gina Hinojosa won the governor's race after promising to keep new data-center approvals paused until the state legislature writes new rules, the Texas Tribune and DatacenterDynamics reported. The stakes, in our prices: since Tuesday's close, cooling-equipment maker Vertiv ($VRT) is **down 25.1%**, Micron ($MU) 23.2% and Nvidia ($NVDA) 12.1%, while Amazon ($AMZN), one of the biggest buyers, is up 3.8%.

## The story in one sentence

If new Democratic governors and legislatures pause or tax new data centers, the projects slip, and the companies that sell them chips, memory, cooling and power book those orders later, which is what their share prices can least afford. Holland's point, as CNBC reported it, was that the market cannot yet put a number on that delay, so it sold first.

The chain runs through states, not Congress. Data centers are permitted, connected to the grid and given tax breaks by states and towns, so the governor's races in Texas, Ohio and Georgia matter more to the buildout than the House.

## The evidence for it

### 1. Texas already has a pause, and the winner wants to keep it

Outgoing Governor Greg Abbott has already told the state's environmental regulator to halt data-center permits until the grid operator, ERCOT, finishes an audit of proposed projects, the Texas Tribune and the Houston Chronicle reported. Hinojosa has said she would extend that pause until the legislature acts, and she takes office on 19 January.

### 2. Texas holds about a quarter of planned capacity

Wells Fargo estimates that about **27%** of planned US data-center capacity, measured in gigawatts, is in Texas, CNBC and The Daily Reporter reported. A pause there alone would touch about a quarter of the national pipeline.

### 3. Goldman puts the at-risk share at 45%

Goldman Sachs analysts estimated this week that Democratic wins at the state and local level are likely to lead to short-term data-center moratoriums in states that hold about **45%** of planned US capacity expansion, including Texas, Georgia and Michigan, Reuters and FourWeekMBA reported. They also flagged lost tax breaks and tighter power and water rules.

### 4. The pushback is spreading beyond Texas

Morgan Stanley said Democrat Amy Acton's win in Ohio creates a significant risk of data-center delays there, CNBC reported, and Keisha Lance Bottoms also campaigned on limits in Georgia, The Daily Reporter reported. In New York, Democrats have proposed a sweeping pause on data-center construction, Yahoo News reported.

## The case against it

The doubters start with who decides. Any federal bill to curb data centers would still need President Trump's signature or a two-thirds vote to override his veto, and the new Congress does not meet until January, Reuters and FourWeekMBA reported. HSBC analysts see more regulation and oversight rather than broad moratoriums, Yahoo Finance and FourWeekMBA reported, and LPL Financial's Jeff Buchbinder told Reuters he expects the buildout to go ahead, slowed only "some."

1. **The buyers have not blinked.** Alphabet kept its 2026 capital spending plan at $195 billion to $205 billion last week and said it expects capex to "increase significantly in 2027," as we reported, and Amazon expects about $220 billion this year. Their shares rose since Election Day, which is not what a market expecting lost AI revenue would do.
2. **Projects can move.** A paused project in Texas can be rebuilt in a state without a pause, HSBC noted; the order for chips and memory slips, it doesn't vanish.
3. **Supply, not permits, was already the limit.** Microsoft's quarterly capex fell short of estimates because "some third-party capacity deliveries" were late, it said last week, and CNBC reported it trimmed its 2026 forecast to $170 billion from $175 billion. The bottleneck before the election was getting buildings and power ready, not demand.

The first point is the strongest: the money is still committed.

## What would change minds

- **ERCOT's audit:** the grid operator's reports, due on 10 December according to the Texas Tribune, will show how much of the Texas pipeline can connect at all. A large cut would make the pause matter more than the election.
- **19 January:** Hinojosa takes office. An order extending the pause, or a call for a special session on data-center rules, would turn a campaign promise into policy.
- **The buyers' 2027 budgets:** Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon and Meta give their next capex plans with their December-quarter results early next year. A cut tied to permits would be the first hard evidence for the story.

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The Texas risk is real and dated: permits are already paused, the new governor wants to keep them paused, and the state holds about a quarter of planned capacity. The weakest link is the jump from Texas to the whole buildout: no buyer has cut a budget, the federal role is small, and projects can move. The number we'll watch is ERCOT's 10 December count of how much Texas capacity can connect.
