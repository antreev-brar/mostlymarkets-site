On Wednesday the cost of insuring Oracle's ($ORCL) debt against default hit a record, and so did the same insurance on Alphabet ($GOOGL), SpaceX and CoreWeave ($CRWV), according to S&P Global Market Intelligence data reported by Reuters. It came two days after Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia ($NVDA) set off a record rally in AI stocks, and it says lenders did not join in. The question now splitting Wall Street: is the credit market seeing a real problem with how the AI build is financed, or reacting to a flood of new bonds?

The number at the center is Oracle's five-year credit default swap spread: **356 basis points**, or $3.56 a year to insure $100 of its debt. CoreWeave's spread is above 1,200 basis points, Reuters and TheNextWeb reported. Oracle stock was down 8.7% at 10:30 a.m. ET in our data, below any close of the past year.

```highlights
The short version
- **What's happening:** Default insurance on Oracle, Alphabet, SpaceX and CoreWeave hit records this week, with Oracle at 356 basis points, even as AI stocks briefly rallied on Huang's return.
- **Why it matters:** Much of the AI build is now paid for with borrowed money, so if lenders charge more, the build slows, and with it the orders of every chip and hardware maker.
- **Our read:** The bears are right about Oracle and the neoclouds; the bulls are right about the giants. The market is selling both as one trade, and Oracle's December report will show which side it has to move toward.
```

## The bear case

### Liz Ann Sonders, chief investment strategist, Charles Schwab
**The sell-off is about returns on borrowed money, not about Nvidia's boss.** Sonders said in a CNBC interview this week that the AI sell-off predated the Huang news, and pointed to the build-out itself: "the spread between the cost of capital and return on invested capital," as reported by CNN and others. Her argument is that rate hikes widen that gap, and Wednesday's 3.3% core inflation reading makes a December hike more likely, as [we reported this morning](/news/2026-11-25/core-pce-inflation-rose-to-3-3-in-october-a-tenth-above-forecasts-before-a-fed-hike/).

### Jordan Chalfin, head of technology credit, CreditSights
**Oracle is an investment-grade name that trades like junk.** Chalfin has called Oracle "the most important IG tech credit" and said it trades more like a high-yield bond, CNBC reported on 19 October. In a note for clients, CreditSights pointed to Oracle's free-cash-flow burn, its need to issue about $70 billion of debt over two and a half fiscal years, downgrade risk at Moody's and its reliance on one customer, OpenAI. Our [breakdown of Oracle](/news/2026-11-21/oracle-orcl-has-a-664-billion-backlog-and-no-free-cash-is-the-business-broken/) put its free cash flow at negative $28.7 billion over the 12 months to August.

### Ruben Roy, analyst, Stifel
**The vendor money that helped the smaller AI clouds won't come back fast.** In a note on Monday, Roy said he does not expect Huang's return to bring a quick return of Nvidia's broader financing for its customers, given scrutiny of how such financing and guarantees are disclosed, Reuters reported in a story carried by Yahoo Finance and Media Selangor. That matters most for borrowers such as CoreWeave, whose bonds and default swaps took the worst of this week's selling.

## The bull case

### Robert Schiffman, senior credit analyst, Bloomberg Intelligence
**The biggest spenders can carry far more debt, and the widening is about supply.** Schiffman has called the hyperscalers the "Mount Rushmore" of credits, with "massive financial flexibility" to borrow hundreds of billions of dollars, Quartz reported. He has argued that their spreads widened because of a surge in new bonds rather than weaker businesses, and that the bubble talk is about stock prices, not debt. He also singles out Oracle, with negative outlooks at S&P and Moody's, as the clearest stress point.

### Oracle's management
**The OpenAI build is paid for, and the cash burn ends on a set date.** Oracle said after the close on 20 November that it had secured all the funding it needs for its OpenAI contract, as [we reported](/news/2026-11-20/oracle-orcl-says-its-openai-data-center-deal-is-fully-funded-after-an-8-drop/). At its investor day on 28 October it said free cash flow turns positive in the second half of fiscal 2028 and tops $30 billion in fiscal 2029, Yahoo Finance and investingLive reported.

## Where they agree
Both sides single out Oracle and the neoclouds, the smaller AI-only clouds, as the weak points, and neither expects a wave of defaults soon. Strategists at Aviva's investment arm put it in the middle this week: near-term default risk is low, but rising debt is squeezing cash flows, so they urged caution on tech bonds, Reuters reported.

## The number that decides it
Oracle's fiscal second-quarter report, for September to November, is expected around 10 December, though the company has not yet set the date. Its free cash flow and capital spending will show whether the burn is shrinking toward the fiscal 2028 turn it promised, and its default swap will show whether lenders believe it.

## Our read
The evidence favors the bears on Oracle and CoreWeave and the bulls on the giants. Oracle's negative $28.7 billion of free cash flow in a year and a 356-basis-point swap are not a supply story, while Reuters noted that the spreads stay manageable for companies with big revenues such as Alphabet. The mistake today is in the stock market, which sold [Amazon](/news/2026-11-25/why-amazon-amzn-stock-is-falling-today/) almost as hard as [Oracle](/news/2026-11-25/why-oracle-orcl-stock-is-down-today/). Oracle's December report is the test: a smaller cash burn would favor the bulls, and a wider one would leave the bears right about the AI borrowers that matter most.
