Two Federal Reserve officials voted on Wednesday to raise interest rates, and Chair Kevin Warsh said energy and related prices "have moved higher again in recent weeks." Both fit Wall Street's story about oil: while the Iran war lasts, crude stays above $100 a barrel, inflation stays high, and the Fed raises rates again in December.

On Friday, Exxon Mobil ($XOM) and Chevron ($CVX) closed at records after their refining profits soared, and US crude ended the week at about **$103 a barrel**.

The stakes: Marathon Petroleum ($MPC), a refiner, has nearly tripled in value this year, by our count, while United Airlines ($UAL), a big buyer of jet fuel, is down 6.8%.

## The story in one sentence

> "Inflation expectations reversed course again in 2026 as the Iran conflict lengthened and energy prices continued to soar." Joanne Hsu, director of the University of Michigan's consumer surveys, in a report on 23 October.

The US and Israel attacked Iran on 28 February; Iran blockaded the Strait of Hormuz, which carried about a fifth of the world's oil before the war, and the US blockaded Iran's ports. If fuel stays expensive, consumers come to expect more inflation, the Fed raises rates to stop that expectation from sticking, and the companies that pump and refine oil keep earning windfall profits while the ones that burn it pay.

## The evidence for it

### 1. Energy prices are rising five times as fast as prices overall

Consumer prices rose **3.7%** in the year to September, the Labor Department reported on 14 October, while energy prices rose 20.4%.

Strip out food and energy and prices rose 2.6%, though that core measure includes airline fares, up 22.4%.

### 2. Consumers expect more inflation, and they blame fuel

US gasoline averaged about **$2.93 a gallon** in the months before the war and was nearing $4.50 again in September, according to AAA figures cited by the University of Michigan. Most consumers expect gas prices to keep rising, which Hsu read as doubt about "a quick resolution to energy supply constraints."

Their long-run inflation expectations rose for a second month in October, to 3.5%, the highest since May, Michigan's final reading showed. That matters because a central bank can wait out an oil shock only if people treat it as a one-time jump in prices, and Hsu found "little evidence" that they do.

She likens the years since the pandemic to the 1960s and 1970s, when supply shocks such as the 1973 oil embargo and the 1978-79 Iranian revolution came in a series, and expectations bottomed out higher after each one.

### 3. Refining is where the money is

Crude is still "getting out of the Gulf," Raphael Portela of the consultancy Wood Mackenzie told CNBC, but the diesel and jet fuel it would have become are largely missing, and Russia's refinery outages and export ban have taken "a second slug out of the global pool." US plants have no slack: Exxon's Gulf Coast refineries ran at 100% of capacity in the third quarter and Chevron's US plants at 98%, CNBC and the Associated Press reported.

US diesel averaged **$6.32 a gallon** on Friday, more than 70% above a year earlier, according to AAA data cited by both outlets.

The profit has moved from the well to the refinery. Chevron's refining profit more than quadrupled from a year earlier to $4.9 billion in the third quarter, while its oil and gas profit fell from the second quarter ([our story](/news/2026-10-30/chevron-cvx-tops-q3-estimates-as-refining-profit-jumps/)). Exxon's refining arm earned $7.2 billion, a little more than its production business, its release shows.

### 4. The Fed is leaning toward another hike

The Fed held its key rate at 3.75% to 4.00% on Wednesday, six weeks after its September hike, but Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack voted for another ([our story](/news/2026-10-28/fed-held-rates-but-two-officials-wanted-a-hike/)). Futures put the odds of a quarter-point hike on 9 December at about **85%** after the meeting, according to Bloomberg data cited by Yahoo Finance.

Seema Shah of Principal Asset Management expects a hike in December and another in early 2027, and said the two dissents "underline that tightening bias," CNBC reported.

### 5. A new round of strikes may follow Tuesday's vote

The Wall Street Journal reported this week that the US has told Gulf allies to prepare for a possible short, intense campaign against Iran after the 3 November midterm elections. Iran has threatened to hit back at economic targets across the region, energy sites among them, The Times of Israel and investingLive reported.

More Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia had already sent Brent crude, the global benchmark, to a $116.72 settlement on 23 October, its highest since 29 April ([our market wrap](/news/2026-10-23/stock-market-today-oil-hits-its-highest-since-april-and-the-dow-drops-653-points/)).

## The case against it

1. **Supply is getting through, and more fuel is coming.** Despite Iran's blockade, production and export flows through Hormuz are "still holding at close to pre-conflict levels," if at elevated shipping rates, MUFG analysts Edward Bell and Soojin Kim said, The National reported on 23 October. Russian refiners are raising diesel output for export under a supply deal with the US, Reuters and CNBC reported this week, and President Donald Trump said on 23 October that the US "could end it very quickly."
2. **Bond investors aren't pricing an inflation spiral.** The 10-year Treasury yield fell **0.35 point** in October, to 4.94%, its biggest monthly drop since December 2023, by the Treasury's daily figures. The 2-year yield, which tracks expected Fed moves most closely, fell almost as much, a sign that bond traders see less tightening ahead than they did a month ago.
3. **Inflation outside energy is tame, and the chair sounds patient.** Core prices rose 2.6% in the year to September, and Warsh said on Wednesday that the Fed's "monetary medicine" was starting to work. Employers added just 29,000 jobs in September, the Labor Department said.

We think the third point is the strongest: an oil shock that hasn't spread into other prices is the kind central banks usually wait out, and a weak job market makes waiting easier. The first is the weakest, because crude kept flowing while fuel ran short.

## What would change minds

- **Tuesday 3 November, and after.** Trump has said he won't strike Iran before the midterms. Renewed strikes, and Iranian attacks on ships or energy sites in reply, would strengthen the story. A Hormuz settlement would weaken it, but not overnight: Portela told CNBC one "would hit margins quickly on sentiment, but with inventories this depleted, the physical tightness carries well into 2027."
- **Friday 6 November.** The October jobs report comes at 8:30 a.m. ET and Michigan's preliminary November reading of inflation expectations at 10 a.m. Another weak jobs month would make a hike harder to justify; another rise in expectations would make waiting harder.
- **Tuesday 10 November, 8:30 a.m. ET.** The Labor Department publishes October's consumer prices. Another big rise in the energy index, or core inflation moving well above 2.6%, would back the hawks before the Fed decides on 9 December.

## The stocks exposed

Refiners are the stocks most tied to the story, and their prices already assume the windfall won't last. Valero ($VLO) earned **more than six times** as much in the third quarter as a year earlier, its results show, but its stock has risen 134% in the past 12 months, by our count.

When the story wobbled on 27 October, as oil fell on hopes raised by talks with Iran and on reports of more Russian diesel, refiners fell 7% to 9% and the airlines rose.

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The fuel shortage is real: refineries are running flat out, Shell's refining margin is more than three times last year's, and energy prices are up 20.4% in a year.

The weakest link is the step from oil to a December hike. Futures put the odds at about 85%, but with core inflation at 2.6% and Warsh saying the Fed's medicine is starting to work, we think December is a closer call than that.

The number we'll watch is core inflation in October's consumer prices, due 10 November: a clear rise would show the oil shock spreading, and would strengthen the hawks' case.
