On Tuesday, Dell Technologies ($DELL) reported the best quarter in its history: revenue more than doubled to $54.9 billion, its AI server backlog reached a record $132.3 billion, and it raised its full-year adjusted EPS forecast to **$29.68 from $25.50**. The stock fell 11.8% that day anyway, and several of the analysts who cover it cut their price targets while keeping their buy ratings.

On Friday the stock **jumped 5.2%** to $312.54 by 10:16 a.m. ET as buyers returned to AI hardware, which leaves it at about 10.5 times this year's guided earnings. The argument is whether that multiple is a mistake or a fair price for a profit peak.

## The bull case

### Mark Newman, analyst, Bernstein
**The quarter was "exceptionally strong" and Dell is his top pick among AI infrastructure suppliers.** Newman kept his Outperform rating after the results, Investing.com and Stocktwits reported on 25 November. He still cut his price target to $580 from $650 by lowering the multiple he applies, to 14 times his fiscal 2029 earnings estimate from 19 times, which Investing.com described as a valuation reset. His argument is that the business is fine and the market is paying less for the whole AI hardware group.

### Aaron Rakers, analyst, Wells Fargo
**AI server orders are still strong enough to carry a weak PC business.** Rakers kept his Overweight rating but cut his target to $400 from $545, Investing.com, Benzinga and MarketBeat reported on 25 November, citing PC demand that was weaker than he expected alongside continued strength in AI server orders. The release backs both halves: AI server revenue rose 291% to $22.0 billion while consumer PC sales fell 15%.

## The bear case

### Tim Long, analyst, Barclays
**Dell's growth is mostly memory prices being passed on, not new AI demand.** Long cut Dell to equal weight from overweight on 14 October, CNBC reported, arguing that infrastructure companies have pushed the higher price of memory onto customers, so their revenue growth reflects memory costs more than enterprise AI spending. When memory prices normalize and then fall, he said, that revenue goes with them. Dell fell about 11% that day.

The numbers give him something to work with. Dell's gross margin over the last two years was 20.3%, against a 43.2% median for the 29 hardware makers we track, and its free cash flow was 5.3% of revenue, against 13.4%, from its SEC filings. A business that passes costs through earns a thin slice of each dollar of revenue, so a revenue boom built on prices can reverse without much warning in the profit line.

## Where they agree
Nobody disputes the backlog: the analysts who cut their targets after the quarter, Bernstein and Wells Fargo among them, kept buy ratings. All three also agree the PC business is weak: Client Solutions revenue rose only 3% and its operating profit fell 6%, as memory costs pushed PC prices up and units down.

The disagreement is about what happens to Dell's revenue per server when memory stops getting dearer. Research firm TrendForce has warned that DRAM price gains could fade by late 2027, 24/7 Wall St. and Invezz reported on Friday.

## The number that decides it
The deciding figure is the margin Dell's server-and-storage unit earns as memory costs change. It was **17.0%** in the third quarter, up from 12.4% a year earlier, and a fall would support the bear; a margin that holds while the backlog grows again would support the bulls. Micron's results on 17 December will show whether memory prices are still rising, and Dell's fourth-quarter report, due in late February, will show the margin.

## Our read
The demand evidence favors the bulls: a backlog about 1.7 times this year's AI server forecast is hard to square with a stock priced at 10.5 times earnings, against a 17.1 times median for its peers. But Long's point about earnings quality is real, and our numbers support it: Dell keeps a fifth of each sales dollar as gross profit, and a revenue boom driven by memory prices can unwind faster than the backlog suggests. At today's price, the market is already assuming much of that unwinding, which makes the margin in February the number to watch, as covered in [our narrative on AI hardware being priced at its peak](/news/2026-11-25/wall-street-is-pricing-ai-hardware-at-its-peak-dells-record-didnt-change-that/) and [our recap of the quarter](/news/2026-11-24/dell-technologies-dell-beats-q3-estimates-as-revenue-doubles-and-its-ai-backlog-hits-132-billion/).
