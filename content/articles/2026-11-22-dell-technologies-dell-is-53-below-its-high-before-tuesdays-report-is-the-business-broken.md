## How it got here

Michael Dell started the company in his University of Texas dorm room in 1984, selling PCs direct to buyers. He took it private in 2013, bought data-storage maker EMC in 2016 for about $67 billion, and brought Dell back to the stock market in 2018, leaving it with a large enterprise business and a lot of debt.

AI turned that enterprise business into the growth engine. Dell builds Nvidia-based server racks for AI labs, chip-renting clouds and companies, and its sales of those AI-optimized servers doubled to $16.4 billion in the quarter to July, its 1 September release said. It raised its full-year revenue forecast to $192 billion, and the stock **soared 15.8%** the next day, 2 September, in our data.

Since then the stock has fallen from $588.40 on 17 September to $278.02 on Friday, 20 November, including an 11.0% drop on Friday alone and a 38.6% fall over the past month in our data. **Is Dell a business the AI slowdown fears have caught out, or one the market has priced for a bust that isn't showing up?**

## The business in one line

Companies, governments, chip-renting clouds and consumers pay Dell for servers, storage and PCs, mostly in one-off purchases tied to upgrade cycles, with services and financing on top.

## The numbers that matter

### Growth
Revenue grew **28.3% a year** over the last two years, above the 16.6% median of the 30 hardware makers we cover, but only 5.5% a year over five years, just below the median. The boom is recent: sales went sideways between fiscal 2022 and fiscal 2025 before AI servers took off.

### Profitability
Dell keeps **20.3 cents of gross profit** per dollar of sales over two years, in the bottom quarter of its peers, against a 43.2% median, because it assembles other companies' chips and memory. The operating margin was 9.4% over the 12 months to July, below most peers, though it has widened 2.9 points in five years.

### Cash
Free cash flow was **5.7% of sales** over the 12 months to July, behind most peers' 13.5%. In the July quarter it fell to $986 million on $47.0 billion of sales, about 2%, by our arithmetic from its 10-Q, as Dell spent cash building servers it hadn't yet been paid for.

### Per-share returns
Diluted shares fell **9.9%** in two years, the biggest drop among the 31 hardware makers we cover, and earnings per share grew 15.5% a year over five years, above the 14.0% median. Buybacks have done a lot of the lifting for shareholders.

### Balance sheet
Net debt was **$22.9 billion** in July, 1.6 years of operating profit, and shareholders' equity was negative $1.4 billion after years of buybacks and the EMC debt. That is manageable while profit is rising, and a squeeze if it falls.

## Why the stock is where it is

1. **AI demand may have peaked or been pulled forward.** Citi's Asiya Merchant named this as the main fear weighing on the stock when she cut her target to $550 from $600 last week while keeping a Buy rating, GuruFocus and Invezz reported.
2. **Memory costs are rising.** Dell buys memory for every server and PC, and Citi listed memory prices eating into profit as a second worry, the same outlets reported ([our memory narrative](/news/2026-11-21/wall-street-is-betting-the-memory-boom-has-peaked-microns-17-december-report-will-test-it/)).
3. **PCs are weakening.** Intel warned on 29 October that memory shortages are choking PC demand, and Dell [fell 12.6% the next day](/news/2026-10-30/why-dell-technologies-dell-stock-is-sinking-today/).
4. **Its biggest AI buyers run on borrowed money.** Dell's 10-Q says most of its AI server revenue comes from "a relatively small number" of large customers and cloud providers, and TD Cowen's Krish Sankar said in September the backlog is levered to the big neoclouds, Stocktwits and Investing.com reported. Those clouds are now [down more than 70% from their highs](/news/2026-11-22/bulls-vs-bears-on-the-ai-sell-off-is-the-bubble-bursting-or-deflating/).

## Testing the worry

**Peak AI demand: not visible yet.** Dell booked $60.9 billion of AI server orders in the quarter to July, against $16.4 billion of AI server sales, so its backlog grew to $95 billion, the release said; TD Cowen said most of it is noncancelable. Nvidia, its supplier, [guided to $130 billion](/news/2026-11-17/nvidia-nvda-tops-q3-estimates-and-guides-to-130-billion-in-its-first-report-without-huang/) of quarterly sales last Tuesday. A pull-forward would show up first as slower new orders, which Tuesday's report will show. *Temporary until the orders say otherwise.*

**Memory costs: contained so far.** Dell's gross margin rose to 20.9% in the July quarter from 17.8% in the April quarter by our arithmetic from its filings, while memory prices were climbing, and ISG's margin nearly doubled. It has been passing costs on. *Contained, but it is a quarter-by-quarter fight.*

**PCs: a small part of the story.** Consumer PCs are 4% of sales, and commercial PC revenue was still growing 22% in the July quarter. A weak PC quarter would dent profit more than the stock. *Contained.*

**Customer funding: the worry that survives.** If chip-renting clouds can't borrow, they can't take delivery, and Dell would be left with servers built on its own cash, which is already thin. This is the risk that could turn a growth problem into a balance-sheet problem. *Lasting, and spreading through the neoclouds.*

## The moat

1. **Scale in buying and building.** Dell had $151 billion of revenue in the 12 months to July, the scale to buy chips and memory in bulk and deliver whole racks quickly. Its operating margin of 9.4% beats Hewlett Packard Enterprise's ($HPE) 6.2% and HP's ($HPQ) 5.4% in our data.
2. **The corporate customer base.** Commercial PCs earned $13.2 billion in the quarter, up 22%, the release said. Those same IT departments buy Dell servers and storage, which gives it a way into enterprise AI that the neocloud specialists lack.
3. **Storage and services.** The EMC business sells storage that AI data needs; storage revenue grew 26% to $4.85 billion in the quarter.

The rivals are close behind on the product: HPE and Super Micro Computer sell similar Nvidia racks. Dell's edge is execution and size, not technology, which is why its gross margin sits in the bottom quarter of hardware makers.

## The risks we'd watch

1. **A neocloud stops paying or cancels.** If a tenth of the $95 billion backlog went, that would be about $9.5 billion of sales, more than half of the July quarter's $16.4 billion of AI server revenue.
2. **Margin squeeze from memory.** Each point of gross margin on $192 billion of forecast revenue is worth about $1.9 billion of profit a year, by our arithmetic, so a two-point squeeze would cost about $3.8 billion a year, close to the July quarter's $4.1 billion net income.
3. **Cash tied up in inventory.** Free cash was about 2% of sales in the July quarter; another quarter like it while debt sits at $22.9 billion would limit buybacks, which have done much of the per-share work.

At 9.7 times expected earnings, the market is pricing Dell like HPE (8.8 times) and HP (9.3 times), companies growing far more slowly. That assumes earnings will fall well short of where Dell is guiding: $49 billion of revenue and $6.50 of adjusted earnings a share for the October quarter, give or take $0.5 billion and 10 cents, according to its September outlook. Analysts expect slightly more, about $6.59 a share on about $49.5 billion, MarketBeat and Barchart reported.

## Our verdict

**Business: Mixed. Price: Below its usual price.** Dell is growing faster than most hardware makers and shrinking its share count faster than any of them, but it keeps a fifth of each sales dollar as gross profit and turns too little into cash, so a turn in AI spending would hit it hard. The trailing P/E of 16.1 times is below its 20.2 times median, and the forward multiple already treats this year's earnings as a peak. What would change our mind is the order book: new AI orders well below the $60.9 billion of the July quarter, or a cut to the $192 billion revenue forecast, would confirm the bears; a backlog that holds near $95 billion would say the stock has priced a bust that hasn't arrived. Tuesday, 24 November, after the close, settles the first part of that.

The tension: Dell's business has never been bigger, and its stock has never priced it more like a company about to shrink.
