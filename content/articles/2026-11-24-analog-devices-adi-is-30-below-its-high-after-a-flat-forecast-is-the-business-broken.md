## How it got here

Analog Devices was founded in 1965 by two MIT graduates, Ray Stata and Matthew Lorber, and its first product was an amplifier, a part that boosts electrical signals. It grew into a maker of the chips that turn real-world signals, such as sound, pressure and temperature, into data, and back again.

It became a giant by buying. It agreed to buy Linear Technology for about $14.8 billion in 2016 and Maxim Integrated in an all-stock deal in 2020, and revenue rose from $3.4 billion in fiscal 2016 to $12.0 billion in fiscal 2022, in its SEC filings. Then the cycle turned: revenue fell 23% in fiscal 2024.

The recovery was fast. Fiscal 2026 revenue rose 38% to a record **$15.2 billion**, the company said on Tuesday, and fourth-quarter sales of $4.37 billion beat estimates ([our recap](/news/2026-11-24/analog-devices-adi-beats-q4-estimates-but-a-flat-q1-forecast-sends-shares-down-8/)). But the forecast for the January quarter was nearly flat, and the stock fell harder than any day in our data. **Is the flat forecast the top of the cycle, or a pause in a business that compounds cash?**

## The business in one line

About 100,000 manufacturers, by the company's count, pay Analog Devices for chips that sit inside their machines for years, buying again every time they build another factory robot, car, test system or data-center rack.

## The numbers that matter

### Growth
Revenue grew **14.5% a year** over five fiscal years, in line with the 14.2% median for the 25 chipmakers we cover, but most of that came from the Maxim deal. Earnings per share grew only 6.8% a year over the same span, in the bottom third of the group, because the company issued shares to pay for Maxim.

### Profitability
The gross margin averaged **63.4%** over the last two years, against a 55.6% median for chipmakers, in our filings data. That is pricing power: analog chips are designed into products for years and are a small part of their cost. The operating margin under standard accounting was 35.5% in the 12 months to August, above the 29.6% median.

### Cash
This is the strongest number. Free cash flow was **35.6% of revenue** in the 12 months to August, in the top quarter of the group, and $5.5 billion, or 36% of revenue, for fiscal 2026, the release said. The company returned $5.4 billion of it through dividends and buybacks.

### Returns
Return on capital is about **9.6%**, below the 17.8% chipmaker median, in our data. The acquisitions, paid for largely in shares, left $33.6 billion of shareholder equity on the books, so the returns on the money originally paid look modest even when the business is running well.

### Balance sheet
Net debt was **$7.0 billion** at 1 August, about 1.4 years of operating profit. That is comfortable for a company producing $5.5 billion of free cash a year.

## Why the stock is where it is

1. **The forecast stalls growth.** Revenue of $4.4 billion, give or take $150 million, is 0.6% above the fourth quarter at the midpoint, and adjusted EPS of $3.82 is below the $3.88 to $3.89 analysts expected, according to Investing.com and Yahoo Finance. A year ago, the first quarter grew 2.6% on the fourth.
2. **Margins step down.** The adjusted operating margin is guided to about 50.5%, nearly 2 points below the fourth quarter's 52.3%.
3. **The whole group sold off.** Texas Instruments ($TXN) was down 9.1%, Microchip ($MCHP) 7.1% and ON Semiconductor ($ON) 7.6% as of 12:15 p.m. ET, in our quotes, as chip stocks gave back Monday's rally ([our story](/news/2026-11-24/amd-credo-and-monolithic-power-shares-slide-what-happened/)).
4. **The bar was high.** The shares had risen 9.5% on Monday and 68% over the past year, to 23.4 times expected earnings, above the chipmaker median ([our preview](/news/2026-11-23/what-to-expect-from-analog-devices-adi-q4-earnings/)).

## Testing the worry

**Is this the top of the cycle?** The best evidence that it isn't a glut yet is the inventory. Distributors' stock is back inside the company's six-to-seven-week model, and management expects it to stay flat this quarter, the call transcripts show. The company's own inventory rose to 165 days because it is deliberately building chip stockpiles ahead of demand, which is a cost if demand turns. *Partly temporary: the channel looks normal, but nobody can see orders beyond a quarter.*

**Is the margin slip lasting?** The chief financial officer tied it to holiday factory shutdowns and higher spending, including a full quarter of costs from the Alif Semiconductor purchase, the transcripts show. Shutdowns recur every winter. *Temporary.*

**Is the damage contained to Analog Devices?** No. The forecast split by market shows communications and industrial still rising, while automotive slips by a low single-digit percentage and consumer falls 10% to 15%, management said on the call. Texas Instruments was down 9.1% on the same morning. *Spreading: this is a sector repricing as much as a company verdict.*

The worry that survives is the cycle. Analog Devices does not control when its customers stop building, and the last time they did, revenue fell 23% in a year.

## The moat

1. **Design wins that last.** A chip designed into a factory machine or a car stays there for the product's life, often many years, so revenue is sticky once won. The proof is in the margin: 63.4% gross over two years, 7.8 points above the chipmaker median.
2. **Breadth.** About 100,000 customers, by the company's count, means no single buyer can squeeze its prices.
3. **Cash through a downturn.** In fiscal 2024, when revenue fell 23%, free cash flow fell only 12%, to $3.1 billion from $3.6 billion, in its filings.

Rivals, from our data: Texas Instruments earns a higher operating margin, 40.8% over 12 months, but converts less to free cash (24.7% over two years) as it spends on new factories. Microchip, NXP ($NXPI) and ON trade at 13.8 to 14.2 times expected earnings, cheaper than Analog Devices.

## The risks we'd watch

1. **A repeat of 2024.** A 23% fall from fiscal 2026's $15.2 billion would take revenue to about $11.7 billion, roughly fiscal 2025's level.
2. **Spending more to build.** The company expects fiscal 2027 capital spending of $1.6 billion to $1.9 billion, up from about $0.7 billion, GuruFocus and the call transcripts report. If operating cash flow stayed at fiscal 2026's $6.2 billion, the top of that range would cut free cash flow to about $4.3 billion; the CFO said on the call that free cash flow should rise instead.
3. **The fastest-growing piece is AI.** Data centers are 13% of sales and growing more than 100%, so a pause in AI spending would hit the part of the business carrying the growth.

## Our verdict

**Business: Solid. Price: Below its usual price.** Analog Devices turns more than a third of its sales into free cash, holds a gross margin well above its peers, and kept producing cash through the last glut. It isn't Strong because its growth has been bought, its returns on capital trail the group, and its revenue swings with a cycle it can't control. The trailing multiple sits about 29% below its median since August 2024, but those earnings are near a cyclical high. What would change our mind is the February report: another flat forecast for the April quarter, or distributor inventory rising above seven weeks, would say the cycle has turned. A great cash machine, priced fairly, at the point in the cycle where cash machines get cheaper.
