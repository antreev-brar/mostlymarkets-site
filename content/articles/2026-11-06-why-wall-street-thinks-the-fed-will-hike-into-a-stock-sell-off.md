Wall Street's story about the Federal Reserve right now is simple: the economy is strong enough and inflation high enough that the Fed will raise rates again in December, even with tech stocks in a correction. Seema Shah, chief global strategist at Principal Asset Management, said after Friday's jobs report that "a December Fed hike remains firmly on the table despite the recent tightening in financial conditions," Yahoo Finance and Mortgage Professional America reported.

The news peg came at 8:30 a.m. ET on Friday. Employers added **165,000 jobs** in October, against the 65,000 economists expected in FactSet's poll, and unemployment fell to 4.1%, the Labor Department reported. Traders pushed the odds of a quarter-point hike at the Fed's 8-9 December meeting back up to about 80% in futures, Reuters reported, from about two-thirds on Thursday, according to IBTimes and Finwire.

The stakes: on Thursday the S&P 500 fell 4.4% and the Nasdaq Composite 5.5%, its worst day since April 2025 ([our market wrap](/news/2026-11-05/stock-market-today-nasdaq-sinks-5-5-into-a-correction-on-midterm-ai-fears/)). A Fed that hikes anyway removes the cushion investors usually count on after a sell-off.

## The story in one sentence

> "A strong rebuttal to fears that the economy is heading into a meaningful slowdown." Seema Shah, Principal Asset Management, on the October jobs report, 6 November.

The chain runs like this: if hiring stays strong, wages keep rising, and with energy pushing prices up, the Fed has to keep tightening to stop inflation from becoming expected. That means higher borrowing costs for homebuilders and home buyers, more pressure on stocks priced on profits far in the future, and a little more interest income for banks.

## The evidence for it

### 1. Hiring sped up instead of slowing

October's 165,000 jobs came with upward revisions: September's gain was raised to 60,000 from 29,000, and August and September together by 84,000, CNN and Morningstar reported. Unemployment fell to 4.1% from 4.2% while more people joined the labor force, so the drop was not people giving up looking. CIBC Capital Markets said the report shows a labor market near full employment, leaving the Fed free to keep its focus on inflation, Mortgage Professional America and Yahoo Finance reported.

### 2. Inflation is above where it was a year ago, on every measure

The Fed's preferred gauge, the personal consumption expenditures price index, rose **3.6%** in the year to September, the most since May, and 3.1% excluding food and energy, Yahoo Finance and Deloitte reported. Consumer prices rose 3.7%, with energy up 20.4% ([our oil narrative](/news/2026-10-31/the-story-wall-street-is-telling-about-100-oil-and-the-fed/)).

Average hourly earnings rose 0.4% in October and 3.1% from a year earlier, both above forecasts, Morningstar reported, and ABC News noted paychecks are still losing ground to inflation. Pay rising slower than prices squeezes households, but faster monthly wage gains are what the Fed watches for a wage-price spiral.

### 3. The Fed has already shown its hand

The Fed raised rates in September to 3.75%-4.00%, its first hike since July 2023, and held there on 28 October, when Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack voted for another hike ([our story](/news/2026-10-28/fed-held-rates-but-two-officials-wanted-a-hike/)). In September a large majority of officials projected another hike before the end of 2026, Chase noted.

## The case against it

The doubters say the sell-off is doing the Fed's job for it. Lee Hardman, senior currency analyst at MUFG, said the tightening of US financial conditions "could prove more important in deciding Fed policy action in December" than Friday's jobs report, Reuters reported via Investing.com and Pound Sterling Live. Peter Boockvar, chief investment officer at One Point BFG Wealth Partners, has argued that a continued slide in tech stocks could keep the Fed from hiking, GuruFocus reported.

1. **Oil, the reason for the hike, is falling.** US crude was **$90.73** a barrel at 10:05 a.m. ET on Friday in our data, down from about $103 on 30 October, a slide that began on 2 November as the US and Iran pursued diplomacy, CNBC and The Wall Street Journal reported. If energy drove the inflation, cheaper energy weakens the case. This is the strongest counterpoint.
2. **Stocks have tightened conditions.** The S&P 500's 4.4% fall on Thursday raised the cost of capital for companies without the Fed moving. Even Capital Economics' Ariane Curtis, who expects a hike, wrote that equity weakness "is contributing to tightness in financial conditions," Pound Sterling Live reported.
3. **Core inflation is not running away.** Core CPI rose 2.6% in the year to September, closer to the Fed's 2% goal than the headline 3.7%.

## What would change minds

- **October CPI, Tuesday 10 November, 8:30 a.m. ET.** A second month of energy prices pulling the headline down, with core near 2.6%, would be the first real crack in the hike case.
- **Oil.** US crude holding near $90 or falling further on a US-Iran deal would undo the main reason two officials wanted a hike in October.
- **Stocks.** Another week like this one, with the Nasdaq sliding deeper into its correction, would test whether the Fed is willing to tighten into falling markets. The minutes of the October meeting, due 18 November, will show how many officials favored moving.

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The labor market and the Fed's own split vote make a December hike the likeliest outcome, and Friday's report took away the argument that the economy was cracking. The weakest link is oil: the case for a second hike was built on $100 crude, and crude is now near $91. The number we'll watch is October's CPI energy index on Tuesday; if it falls and core stays near 2.6%, the 80% odds are too high.
