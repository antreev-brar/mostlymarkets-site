## How it got here

Lilly spent most of its 150 years as a steady Indianapolis drugmaker built on insulin, then Prozac and cancer drugs. Its revenue was **$24.5 billion in 2020** and barely grew for the next two years, according to its SEC filings.

The turning point was tirzepatide, a weekly injection that mimics two gut hormones that control blood sugar and appetite. Sold as Mounjaro for diabetes and Zepbound for obesity, it took revenue to $65.2 billion in 2025 and $86.5 billion in the 12 months to September.

This week added two pieces. On Wednesday the stock set a closing high of $1,417.99, and on Friday Lilly agreed to sell Zepbound and its new pill, Foundayo, through the Hims & Hers ($HIMS) telehealth site at its own cash prices, Reuters and MarketWatch reported. So: **can a company this dependent on one molecule keep growing fast enough to justify a trillion-dollar price?**

## The business in one line

Insurers, governments and, more and more, patients paying cash buy Lilly's medicines every week or every day, for as long as the patient stays on them.

## The numbers that matter

### Growth
Revenue grew **21.6% a year over five fiscal years** to 2025, the fastest of 18 drugmakers in our coverage, where the median is 8.1%. In the third quarter it grew 38.6%, against an 8.5% median.

### Profitability
The gross margin, what's left after making the drugs, was 83.4% over two years, against a 72.6% median. Net profit was 31.9% of revenue in the 12 months to September.

### Per share
Earnings per share grew 27.6% a year over five years, double the 13.4% median, and the diluted share count fell 1.3% in two years.

### Returns
Return on equity was **68.5%**, in the top tenth of drugmakers, against a 10.8% median. Part of that is debt: equity is a modest $40.3 billion against the size of the business.

### Balance sheet
Net debt was $44.6 billion on 30 September, about 1.6 years of the last 12 months' net profit. That is room, not strain, even as Lilly commits more than $50 billion to US plants.

## Why the stock is where it is

The stock is up 26.0% this year and closed Friday at $1,354.00. The worried investor's list:

1. **Prices are falling fast.** Realized prices fell 15% worldwide in the third quarter and 29% outside the US, mainly because Mounjaro joined China's national insurance list, the release showed.
2. **One molecule is two-thirds of sales.** Mounjaro and Zepbound made 66% of the quarter's revenue.
3. **The pill started slowly.** Foundayo's $184 million of third-quarter sales missed the roughly $217 million analysts expected, Reuters reported. Novo Nordisk ($NVO) got its own obesity pill to market first.
4. **2026 had help that won't recur.** About $2 billion of this year's revenue came from milestones and rebate-estimate changes that won't repeat in 2027, management said on the 29 October call, according to call summaries from Bullfincher and Betafinch.

## Testing the worry

**Falling prices: lasting, but so far contained.** Volume rose 53% in the third quarter, more than three times the price decline, and management expects net price to stay a headwind in 2027 with volume more than offsetting it, according to the call summaries. Medicare's GLP-1 Bridge program, which began paying for obesity drugs on 1 July, already covers more than half a million people, and about 70% of new patients start on a Lilly drug, management said. Government buyers pay less per prescription, so that growth also pulls the average price down.

**Concentration: lasting, but spreading the right way.** Lilly plans to file retatrutide, a weekly injection that mimics three hormones, with the FDA in the first quarter of 2027, after it produced weight loss of up to 22.6% at 80 weeks in its TRIUMPH-3 trial, HCPLive and Indian Pharma Post reported. A successor drug from the same lab extends the franchise; it doesn't diversify it.

**The slow pill: temporary, probably.** Foundayo had to answer an FDA request for more safety data after its April approval, FiercePharma and Clinical Trials Arena reported. A third-quarter miss of about $33 million is small against $24.4 billion of revenue, and Friday's Hims & Hers deal adds another way to reach cash-paying patients at $149 a month and up.

**The one-time help: temporary, and known.** $2 billion is about 2% of this year's $91 billion to $92 billion revenue forecast, Lilly's third raise of the year.

The worry that survives is price. It is the one number Lilly doesn't control.

## The moat

1. **Manufacturing scale.** Lilly's US manufacturing commitments since 2020 exceed $50 billion, including $27 billion for four new sites announced in February, Pharma Manufacturing and Business Facilities reported. Making injectable peptides at this volume takes years to copy.
2. **The best-selling drug in the class.** Lilly's US incretin franchise has led new prescriptions for obesity and diabetes since 2025, and Mounjaro alone brought in $10.7 billion in the third quarter, up 65%.
3. **The next drug is ready.** Retatrutide's filing is months away; Pfizer ($PFE) plans to launch its first obesity drug, from its Metsera purchase, in 2028, after 10 Phase 3 studies, BioSpace and Fintool reported.
4. **A direct line to patients.** LillyDirect and now Hims & Hers sell Zepbound for cash at $299 a month, around the insurers.

The rival that matters, Novo Nordisk, trades at 12.2 times forward earnings in our data, a third of Lilly's multiple, which shows how much more growth the market expects from Lilly.

## The risks we'd watch

- **Price.** Mounjaro and Zepbound run at about $64 billion a year on third-quarter sales; every extra five points of price decline costs about $3.2 billion of revenue, roughly 3.5% of this year's forecast.
- **Washington.** The Medicare Bridge program's future after 2026 is not settled; Chief Executive David Ricks was optimistic on the call about moving coverage into Medicare Part D in 2028, but that isn't law.
- **Safety.** Any new safety finding on the class, or on Foundayo, would hit the two-thirds of sales that share one mechanism.

To reach the drug-industry median of 18.2 times forward earnings at today's price, Lilly's earnings would have to rise about 84%. At the 27.6% a year it managed over the last five years, that takes between two and three years; at half that pace, about five.

## Our verdict

**Business: Strong. Price: Below its usual price.** Lilly grows faster than any drugmaker we cover, keeps 83 cents of each sales dollar after production costs and earns returns most of the industry can't match, and the stock trades at 43.9 times trailing earnings against a two-year median of 50.7. What would change our mind is price falling faster than volume grows; the first clean read on that comes with fourth-quarter results and the 2027 forecast in early February, when the one-time items drop out. Retatrutide's filing in the first quarter of 2027 is the next date that could lengthen the runway.

It's the best business in pharma, priced as if the hardest part, keeping growth ahead of falling prices, is already done.
