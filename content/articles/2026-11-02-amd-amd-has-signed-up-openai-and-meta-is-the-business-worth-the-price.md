*AMD is a solid business that has won the two biggest AI buyers outside Nvidia's camp, but at 43 times forward earnings its price assumes those deals turn into Nvidia-like profits; Tuesday's forecast for the Helios ramp is the first real test.*

AMD ($AMD) was down 3.1% at $489.36 as of 11:30 a.m. ET on Monday, a day before it reports third-quarter results after the close on Tuesday, 3 November. That leaves the stock **25% below its closing high** of $649.42 from 6 October, after a 10.9% fall on Friday that came with the selloff in chip stocks over Intel's weak forecast.

## How it got here

AMD spent most of the last decade as the challenger to Intel in PC and server processors, and it won: revenue went from $6.7 billion in 2019 to $34.6 billion in 2025, its filings show. In 2022 it bought Xilinx, the maker of programmable chips, which gave it the embedded business and a large yearly accounting charge.

The turning point for the stock came in October 2025, when OpenAI agreed to deploy up to 6 gigawatts of AMD Instinct AI chips, starting with 1 gigawatt of MI450 chips in the second half of 2026, Tom's Hardware and The Register reported. In February 2026 Meta Platforms ($META) signed a similar 6-gigawatt deal, including Helios, AMD's rack-scale system that sells its AI chips as a whole server rack, Network World and HotHardware reported. On 27 October AMD said Helios racks are running at Meta.

The stock more than doubled this year to its October high, then lost a quarter of its value in four weeks. **Is AMD becoming a real second supplier of AI computing, or is the market paying for profits it has not yet shown?**

## The business in one line

Cloud companies, PC makers and console makers pay AMD for processors and AI accelerators, mostly in large, repeat orders each product generation.

## The numbers that matter

All figures are from AMD's SEC filings via our data, judged against the 25 chipmakers we cover.

### Growth
Revenue grew **28.8% a year** over the five fiscal years to 2025, against a peer median of 14.2%, which puts AMD in the top quarter. In the latest quarter it grew 50.1%, above the 36.5% median. *Verdict: strong.*

### Profitability
AMD's operating margin was **15.7%** over the 12 months to June, against a peer median of 29.6%, in the bottom quarter. Part of the gap is accounting: amortization of acquired intangibles, mostly from Xilinx, was about $2.3 billion in 2025, its annual report shows. Adding that back lifts 2025's operating margin from 10.7% to about 17%, still far below Nvidia ($NVDA) at 65.2% or Broadcom ($AVGO) at 48.0%. *Verdict: the weak spot.*

### Cash
Free cash flow was **20.3% of sales** over the last 12 months, or $8.4 billion, against a peer median of 23.6%. Over two years it was 17.6%, in the bottom tenth of the group. *Verdict: improving, but below average.*

### Returns
Return on capital is about **7.8%**, against a peer median of 18.8%. The goodwill and intangibles from the Xilinx deal swell the capital base; thin margins do the rest. *Verdict: weak.*

### Balance sheet
AMD had **$1.9 billion of net cash** at 27 June: $5.1 billion of cash against $3.2 billion of debt. *Verdict: strong; it needs no outside money for the AI build.*

## Why the stock is where it is

Told from the worried investor's side:

1. **Beats that weren't big enough.** On 4 February AMD's shares fell 17.3%, after its first-quarter revenue forecast of $9.8 billion beat the consensus but fell short of hopes above $10 billion, Motley Fool and Seeking Alpha reported. On 5 August they fell 7.0% after a third-quarter forecast of about $13 billion, above the $12.52 billion LSEG consensus but short of hopes for $14 billion, CNBC reported.
2. **The chip selloff.** On Friday AMD fell 10.9%, its worst day since February, as Intel's weak forecast and Amazon's spending plans led investors to sell the chip stocks that had run furthest. We covered it [here](/news/2026-10-30/amd-arm-and-qualcomm-shares-slide-what-happened/).
3. **Profits lag the hype.** The AI deals are sized in gigawatts, but AMD's operating margin is half the peer median and its trailing P/E is 130.

## Testing the worry

**Beats that weren't big enough: temporary.** This is about expectations, not the business. Both forecasts were above consensus, and the results then delivered: second-quarter adjusted EPS of $1.66 topped the $1.62 analysts expected, according to LSEG data cited by CNBC. The risk repeats on Tuesday, though: Jefferies expects AMD to guide to $17 billion for the December quarter, above a consensus of about $16.3 billion, according to the [preview we published last week](/news/2026-10-29/amd-amd-reports-earnings-on-tuesday-what-to-watch/). A forecast that is merely above consensus has not been enough twice this year.

**The chip selloff: temporary for AMD.** Intel's problem was PCs, where high memory prices are hurting sales, as we covered in our [Intel breakdown](/news/2026-10-31/intel-intc-lost-28-6-in-a-day-is-the-business-broken/). AMD's PC business grew 23% last quarter. Friday said more about how far the stocks had run than about AMD's orders.

**Profits lag the hype: the worry that survives.** The test is margin. AMD's GAAP gross margin was 53.8% last quarter, by our data, against Nvidia's 75.0% in its quarter to July, by our data, and its forecast for the third quarter was an adjusted gross margin of about 56%. If Helios racks, which include memory and networking AMD buys from others, carry a lower margin than chips alone, revenue could rise faster than profit. Nobody outside AMD knows that number yet.

## The moat

1. **The two AI buyers that want a second supplier.** OpenAI and Meta have together agreed to deploy up to 12 gigawatts of AMD chips over several product generations, and AMD said each gigawatt is worth tens of billions of dollars in revenue, Tom's Hardware reported. No other chipmaker outside Nvidia has deals of that size.
2. **Processors.** AMD's PC and server chips have taken share from Intel for years. Its client revenue grew 23% last quarter while Intel's revenue grew 5.1% a year over two years and its operating margin was 2.9%, by our data.
3. **A clean balance sheet.** Net cash of $1.9 billion against Intel's need to fund its factories.

The rival that matters is Nvidia: its two-year revenue growth was 77.4% a year and its operating margin 65.2%, against AMD's 33.2% and 15.7%. Nvidia's software, CUDA, remains the default for AI developers. In ten years AMD will very likely still be here and larger; whether it will be more profitable depends on the AI business. *Verdict: a real moat in processors, a promising one in AI.*

## The risks we'd watch

1. **Dilution from the warrants.** OpenAI and Meta each hold a warrant for up to 160 million AMD shares at a penny each, vesting as deployments are reached, Tom's Hardware and Network World reported; OpenAI's last tranche also needs a $600 share price. Worst case, all 320 million vest: against 1.63 billion shares outstanding, that is 19.6% more shares, cutting earnings per share by about 16%. The offset is that full vesting requires 12 gigawatts of purchases, at tens of billions of dollars a gigawatt on AMD's own description. It is a cost of the deals, not a threat to them.
2. **Concentration.** Two customers' deployments are most of the AI story. A delay at either would show up in the data center segment, which was 58% of revenue last quarter.
3. **The Helios ramp.** Helios is due to ramp up in the fourth quarter, and Jefferies has cautioned that initial deployments could face delays, according to our preview.

Put in proportion: the warrants dilute only if AMD sells what the deals promise, and the ramp risk is about timing. The margin question matters more than any of them.

## Our verdict

**Business: Solid. Price: Above its usual price.** AMD grows faster than most chipmakers, has net cash, and has won two of the largest AI buyers as customers for years of product. Its weakness is profit: an operating margin half the peer median, even after the Xilinx accounting is added back. At 129.8 times trailing earnings, against a median of 115.4 since mid-2024, the price is paying for margins AMD has not yet shown. What would change our mind is a gross-margin forecast at or above last quarter's alongside the Helios ramp; Tuesday's report after the close is the first chance to see it. The business is winning; the price assumes it has already won.
