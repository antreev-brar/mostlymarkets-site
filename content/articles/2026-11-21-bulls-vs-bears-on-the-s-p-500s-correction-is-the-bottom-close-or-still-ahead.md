The S&P 500 closed in a correction on Friday, **down 3.7% to 6,910.39** and more than 10% below its 6 October record after seven straight losses, its longest losing streak since February 2020, Reuters and CNBC reported. That leaves Wall Street with one question for Thanksgiving week: is this the last leg of a pullback, or the start of something bigger?

The number both sides are arguing about is how much is already priced. Friday's close is 2.7% below the bottom of the 7,100 to 7,300 range where Morgan Stanley's strategists said in early November the decline would end, while Apple ($AAPL), the market's biggest safe haven, is only 3.9% below its high by our data.

- **Why it matters:** Bulls see panic and history on their side (only five of 15 corrections since 1983 became bear markets); bears see a market that hasn't yet washed out and an AI repricing that is about earnings, not mood.

## The bull case

### Binky Chadha, chief global strategist, Deutsche Bank
**The sell-off has run ahead of the fundamentals.** Chadha told CNBC on Friday that stocks were "very oversold," arguing that economic growth and corporate earnings remain strong, as CNBC and Fortune reported. His point is that a market down this hard on positioning and fear, rather than on falling profits, tends to recover once the selling exhausts itself.

### Chris Larkin, head of trading, E*Trade from Morgan Stanley
**History says most corrections stop short of a bear market.** Larkin said the sell-off looks "highly sentiment driven given the economic backdrop," CBS News and Fortune reported, and pointed to E*Trade's study of corrections from record highs since 1983: only five of the past 15 turned into bear markets, a fall of 20% or more. On those odds, a correction is more often a buying moment than the start of a slump.

### Mike Wilson, chief US equity strategist, Morgan Stanley
**The correction is in its final phase, with a year-end rally to follow.** In a note in early November, reported by Quartz and Investing.com, Wilson wrote that the correction was "entering its final phase" as the S&P 500 "catches down to breadth," with a downside range of 7,100 to 7,300, and kept his year-end target of 8,000. The weakness in his case is now plain: Friday's close took the index below his range, which leaves his thesis needing a quick rebound to stay intact.

## The bear case

### Jonathan Krinsky, chief market technician, BTIG
**The final low hasn't come, because the market hasn't washed out.** Krinsky told clients this week that about half of S&P 500 stocks were still above their 50-day moving averages, against a median of 11% at the bottom of past pullbacks of 8% or more, Seeking Alpha and GuruFocus reported. He also told CNBC that Apple's median drawdown at past final bottoms has been 23%, and that "it would be historically unprecedented to see a final bottom on the S&P with Apple where it is right now." He expects a short-term floor near 7,000, now broken, and a bounce, but not the low.

### Richard Saperstein, chief investment officer, Treasury Partners
**This is an AI repricing, not just panic.** Saperstein said on Friday that the market is repricing for slowing data-centre buildouts, fewer sources of money for AI projects and a slower path for tech earnings growth, TheStreet and Stocktwits reported. If he is right, the falls are tracking real cuts to the profits of the companies that make up most of the index's gains, which panic-based recoveries don't fix. He conceded that forced selling could be exhausted enough for a short-term technical rebound.

### Peter Boockvar, chief investment officer, Bleakley Financial Group
**The Fed won't rescue stocks until the damage is bigger.** Boockvar argued that if the sell-off and doubts about AI spending continue, he doesn't see how the Fed could raise rates again after December, "if they even do then," 24/7 Wall St. and Seeking Alpha reported. That is cold comfort for bulls: it means relief from the Fed depends on the AI slowdown getting worse first, while futures still put the odds of a 9 December hike at about 92%, according to the same reports.

## Where they agree

Both sides agree the selling is concentrated in AI and chip stocks, and that a short-term bounce is likely: Krinsky sees a 4% to 5% recovery toward the 200-day moving average, Saperstein sees forced selling running out, and Wilson's whole case rests on one. The argument is over what comes after the bounce.

## The number that decides it

Breadth is the gauge both camps cite. Wilson's case is that the index is catching down to the weak average stock; Krinsky's is that the average stock hasn't fallen enough. A capitulation would show up as the share of S&P 500 stocks above their 50-day averages dropping toward Krinsky's 11% median, ideally with Apple finally falling. The next fundamental tests are the Fed on 9 December and Micron's results on 17 December.

## Our read

On timing, the bears have the better evidence today: the index has already broken through Wilson's floor and Krinsky's 7,000, and the stock that historically falls last, Apple, is still within 4% of its record. The bulls are right that this is a sell-off in AI expectations, not a broad economic break, which is why Larkin's odds favor a correction over a bear market.
