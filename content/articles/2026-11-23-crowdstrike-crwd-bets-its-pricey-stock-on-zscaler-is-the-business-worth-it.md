## How it got here

George Kurtz, a former chief technology officer at McAfee, co-founded CrowdStrike in 2011 on one idea: protect laptops and servers with a single lightweight program that reports to the cloud, instead of the bulky antivirus software companies installed on every machine. It listed on the Nasdaq in June 2019 and has since added identity, cloud and log-analysis products sold through the same program, called Falcon.

Two shocks tested it. On 19 July 2024 a faulty Falcon update crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers, by Microsoft's count, grounding flights and prompting Delta Air Lines to sue for more than $500 million. Then in February 2026 the stock lost 17.0% in two sessions after Anthropic launched an AI tool that hunts for code vulnerabilities, as investors asked whether AI would make security software cheaper to replace.

On Monday CrowdStrike answered with both hands: a record **$419.2 million** of net new annual recurring revenue (ARR, the yearly value of its subscriptions) for the quarter to October, and its biggest deal by far, for Zscaler ($ZS). **Is CrowdStrike a business worth owning at a price this high, and does the Zscaler deal make it better or just bigger?**

## The business in one line

Companies and governments pay CrowdStrike ($CRWD) a yearly subscription, usually per protected device or user, to detect and stop attackers, and they add modules to the same program as they go.

## The numbers that matter

### Growth
Revenue grew **40.6% a year** over the five fiscal years to January 2026, the top tenth of the 32 software companies we track, whose median is 15.9%. The pace has slowed to about 24% a year over the last two years, still in the top quarter, and the quarter just reported was faster at 28%.

### Profitability
Under standard accounting (GAAP) CrowdStrike barely breaks even: an operating loss of **$0.8 million** in the quarter, against a $62.2 million loss a year earlier. Its operating margin over the 12 months to July was -2.2%, in the bottom quarter of its peers, whose median is 9.7%.

### Cash
Free cash flow was a record **$474.4 million** in the quarter, up from $295.9 million a year earlier, or 30% of revenue. That puts it just above the peer median over two years (27.9% against 27.7%), behind Palo Alto Networks ($PANW) at 36.2% and Fortinet ($FTNT) at 37.8%.

### The gap between the two
The difference between a break-even income statement and a 30% cash margin is mostly pay in stock, which the company excludes from its adjusted numbers. That cost shows up in the share count instead: diluted shares rose **3.9%** in two years, against a peer median of 0.3%.

### Balance sheet
Cash was $5.37 billion at the end of October. At the end of July it held **$4.3 billion** more cash than debt, so the business needs no outside money, and the Zscaler deal is paid in shares rather than borrowing.

## Why the stock is where it is

The shares are up 95.8% this year to Friday's close, yet 26.0% below their October high. From a worried holder's seat, three things put it there:

1. **AI could cheapen security software.** Anthropic's code-scanning tool set off a cyber-stock sell-off in February, Finviz and Barchart reported; CrowdStrike's lowest close of the year, $87.56, came on 24 February.
2. **The multiple.** On 5 November it fell 9.9% with no company news, as the Nasdaq-100 dropped and investors sold the most expensive AI winners.
3. **The deal.** On Monday it slid 10.1% by 12:15 p.m. ET while Zscaler rose 16.5%: holders are paying a 34% premium in their own shares.

## Testing the worry

**AI replacing security: so far, temporary.** If AI tools were eating demand, new business would slow. It did the opposite: net new ARR rose 58% from a year earlier, and ARR growth sped up to 27% from 23% at the same point last year, when ARR was $4.92 billion. AI-written code and AI agents add things to protect; the evidence so far says that effect is bigger.

**The outage: contained.** Fifteen months after crashing 8.5 million machines, ARR growth was 23%; a year after that it is faster. Customers stayed, though the lesson about how much damage one update can do still matters (see the risks).

**The deal: the math works, the execution is the test.** Zscaler sells zero-trust network security, which checks every user before letting them reach company systems, a product CrowdStrike didn't have. CrowdStrike is valuing it at about **51 times** Zscaler's $852 million of free cash flow over the 12 months to July, while its own shares trade at about 119 times. Swapping expensive stock for cheaper cash flow lifts cash per share: on the two companies' trailing numbers, free cash flow per share goes from about $1.73 to about $2.15, a 25% gain before any savings. That is why the company says the deal adds to free cash flow per share from day one.

The bear case is about everything after that. JPMorgan analyst Brian Essex wrote that the deal would dilute CrowdStrike's growth and free cash flow margins, as TheFly and Stocktwits reported; Zscaler grew 24.9% in its latest quarter and keeps 25.4% of sales as free cash, both below CrowdStrike. Jefferies, which kept its buy rating, said the combination would be a "more formidable" rival to Palo Alto Networks but flagged the risk of regulatory scrutiny, Yahoo Finance and Investing.com reported. The deal isn't expected to close until the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal 2028, August 2027 to January 2028.

**The worry that survives is the price.** The business answered the first two worries in its numbers; the third is a bet on execution that the stock is already paying for.

## The moat

1. **One program, many modules.** Customers add modules to software already on their machines, so each sale costs less than the last. The evidence is the record $419.2 million of net new ARR in a quarter when total ARR grew 27%.
2. **Trust that survived its worst test.** Few vendors could crash 8.5 million computers and grow faster two years later. That suggests switching costs are high: ripping out security software means retraining staff and risking gaps.
3. **Data at scale.** Every protected device feeds the same cloud, so an attack seen at one customer teaches the system for all of them. Rivals have this too, which makes it an entry ticket more than a lead.

The rivals: Palo Alto Networks grew revenue 22.6% a year over two years with a 36.2% free cash margin; Fortinet grew 17.5% with a 33.0% operating margin; Zscaler grew 24.4%. Microsoft ($MSFT) bundles its Defender security software into its business software licenses, which caps what CrowdStrike can charge smaller customers.

## The risks we'd watch

- **The multiple shrinking.** At its median quarter-end multiple since mid-2024, about 90 times free cash flow, CrowdStrike's $1.77 billion of trailing free cash flow would be worth about $159 billion, against a market value of about $211 billion. That's a 25% fall with no change in the business.
- **The 15.9% handed over.** If the targeted $1 billion of extra yearly revenue within 30 months and $500 million of yearly cost savings arrive late, CrowdStrike holders will have given away a sixth of the company for Zscaler's slower growth. The long wait to close gives Palo Alto Networks a year to court Zscaler's customers.
- **One update, every customer.** Falcon runs deep inside the operating system, which is what makes it effective and what let one faulty update in July 2024 take down 8.5 million machines. A repeat would hit a company with more than $10 billion of ARR after the deal, the companies say, and a bigger base.

Here is what 119 times free cash flow asks for. For the multiple to fall to 25 times, about where Microsoft trades on forward earnings, without the share price falling, free cash flow would have to rise almost fivefold, to about $8.4 billion. At 25% a year that takes about seven years. The current price assumes CrowdStrike keeps growing that fast, through a merger, for most of a decade.

## Our verdict

**Business: Solid. Price: Above its usual price.** CrowdStrike grows faster than almost any software company its size, its cash flow is real, and it came through an outage and an AI scare with faster growth than before. It isn't Strong yet because its GAAP profit is near zero and its share count keeps rising, so holders pay for the growth through dilution, now 15.9% more of it. What would change our mind is a sign that the deal costs growth: net new ARR falling below a year earlier in the quarters before it closes. The next test is its fourth-quarter report, for the three months to January. The tension in one line: a great machine for growth, priced as if nothing can go wrong for seven years.
