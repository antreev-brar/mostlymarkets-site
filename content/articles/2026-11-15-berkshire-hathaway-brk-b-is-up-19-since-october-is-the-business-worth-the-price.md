## How it got here

Berkshire Hathaway began as a failing New England textile mill that Warren Buffett bought control of in 1965 and turned into a holding company. Insurance premiums, collected before claims are paid, gave him money to invest: first in stocks, later in whole companies such as the BNSF railroad and a string of electric utilities.

The turning point was this year. Greg Abel, who ran the non-insurance businesses, took over as chief executive on 1 January 2026, with Buffett staying on as chairman. Under Abel, Berkshire resumed buybacks: **$14.4 billion** in the first nine months of 2026, $9.6 billion of it in the third quarter, CNBC and Yahoo Finance reported.

Then the market came to it. The B shares rose on all five trading days last week to a record, and have gained 19.1% since 30 September while the S&P 500 slipped about 1.2%, NewsBytes and 24/7 Wall St. reported, a run that came with no new deal or filing to explain it. **Is the business good enough to justify paying a record price for it?**

## The business in one line

Berkshire is paid insurance premiums by drivers and companies, freight fees by shippers on BNSF, and regulated power bills by utility customers, every month, and invests the cash those businesses throw off.

## The numbers that matter

Our usual peer group for Berkshire, payment firms, exchanges and brokers, doesn't fit a company that owns a railroad, utilities and an insurer, so we judge it against its own record and against the insurer and railroad that compete with its biggest units.

### Growth

Revenue grew **8.6% a year** over five years, to $371.4 billion in 2025, and 11.2% in the latest quarter against a year earlier, in our data. The two years before 2026 were flat, at $371.4 billion in both 2024 and 2025, so this year's pickup is recent.

### Profitability

Operating earnings rose **8.0%** to $37.0 billion in the first nine months of 2026, CNBC and StockAnalysis reported, even after the third-quarter drop. The weak spot is GEICO, where underwriting profit fell to $982 million from $1.77 billion as its loss ratio rose to about 76.6%, Benzinga and Beinsure reported.

### Cash

Free cash flow was **5.8% of revenue** over the past 12 months, in our data, low because BNSF and the utilities spend heavily on track, lines and plants. The cash that matters sits on the balance sheet: $352.3 billion excluding the railroad and utilities, down 1.9% from June, StockAnalysis and Pomegra reported.

### Returns

Return on equity was **10.1%** over the past 12 months, in our data, a figure that includes investment gains. That is well below Progressive's ($PGR) 33.7% and Chubb's ($CB) 15.3%, because more than a quarter of Berkshire's market value, about $1.3 trillion at 1.7 times book, sits in cash and Treasury bills earning a bond yield.

### Balance sheet

Book value was **$761 billion** on 30 September, in our data, and the insurance float, money held for future claims, stood at about $178.5 billion, up $2.1 billion this year, according to the 10-Q as reported by GuruFocus and Benzinga. Because underwriting was profitable, Berkshire was paid to hold that float.

## Why the stock is where it is

From the angle of a worried owner, three things stand out:

1. **The rally has no deal behind it.** 24/7 Wall St. found no Berkshire press release or filing between 6 October and 4 November, the day the shares jumped 3.95% to their first record close of the run, the biggest one-day gain since April 2025 in our data. The run lines up with the AI sell-off: Nvidia ended the same week at a 52-week low.
2. **Earnings fell in the latest quarter.** Operating earnings fell 5.8% in the third quarter, led by a 43% drop in underwriting profit.
3. **The price has moved past where management bought.** Abel's record buyback happened in a quarter when the B shares closed between $488.35 and $529.42; Friday's close was 12.0% above the top of that range.

## Testing the worry

**The GEICO slowdown is temporary and contained.** The drop came from a higher loss ratio at one unit, and nine-month underwriting profit was still $4.8 billion, against $5.7 billion a year earlier, GuruFocus and Beinsure reported. The rest of Berkshire's insurance stayed profitable, so the float still cost nothing.

**The fall in investment income is lasting and spreading.** Insurance investment income fell 14% in the quarter even with Treasury yields high,, and the cash count fell 1.9% in the quarter that Berkshire spent $9.6 billion on buybacks. If rates fall, the income on $352 billion falls with it: each percentage point is about $3.5 billion a year before tax, roughly a quarter of one quarter's operating earnings.

**The price worry is the one that survives.** The railroad and utilities grew 19% and 8%, so the operating businesses are fine. What has changed is what buyers pay: about 1.7 times book value, after Berkshire's own buyer stepped in at third-quarter prices 11% to 18% lower.

## The moat

- **Cheap money from insurance.** A $178.5 billion float that costs nothing while underwriting is profitable; Chubb, a large rival, trades at 2.0 times book in our data with a 15.3% return on equity.
- **A railroad that can't be copied.** BNSF's operating earnings rose 19% in the quarter. Union Pacific ($UNP), its western rival, runs a 40.1% operating margin in our data, the bar BNSF is judged against.
- **Regulated utilities.** Berkshire Hathaway Energy's earnings rose 8% on rates set by regulators, the steadiest income in the group.
- **Cash when nobody else has it.** $352 billion lets Berkshire buy when others must sell, the advantage its buyers are paying up for.

## The risks we'd watch

1. **Interest rates turning down.** A one-point fall in yields on $352 billion of cash costs about $3.5 billion a year in pre-tax income, by our arithmetic.
2. **GEICO's loss ratio.** It rose to about 76.6% in the third quarter and GEICO's underwriting profit nearly halved. Group underwriting is already $0.9 billion behind last year after nine months; a fourth quarter like the third would widen that gap.
3. **The rotation reversing.** The B shares have moved 5% or more on none of the last 252 sessions in our data; a stock that steady can still give back a 19% run quickly if money flows back into AI after Nvidia's report on Tuesday.

## What the price assumes

| Measure | Now | Its history | Peers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price to book | 1.7x | Q3 buybacks at closes of $488 to $529 a share | Chubb 2.0x, JPMorgan 2.5x, Progressive 4.0x |
| Share price | $592.94 | Q3 average close about $505 | 12.0% above the Q3 high |
| Return on equity, 12 months | 10.1% | Includes investment gains | Chubb 15.3%, JPMorgan 17.7% |
| Forward P/E | 55.2x in our data | Distorted by investment gains; not a fair test | JPMorgan 14.2x |

At 1.7 times book, a buyer earning Berkshire's 10.1% return on equity gets about 6% a year on the price (10.1 divided by 1.7), close to what a 10-year Treasury pays at 5.14%. The price assumes that return goes up, through more buybacks or a big acquisition with the cash.

## Our verdict

**Business: Solid. Price: Above its usual price.** The railroad and utilities are growing, the float is free money, and $352 billion of cash is a real option on the next crisis. But returns on equity of about 10% trail the best insurers and banks, operating earnings fell last quarter, and the B shares now trade 12% above the highest price of the quarter in which Abel bought back a record $9.6 billion. What would change our mind is a fourth-quarter report, due with the annual report early next year, showing GEICO's underwriting recovering and buybacks continuing at these prices. Berkshire has become the place investors hide from AI, and hiding has got expensive.
