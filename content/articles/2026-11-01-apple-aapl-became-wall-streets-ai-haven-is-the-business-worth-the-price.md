## How it got here

Apple ($AAPL) sold its first computer in 1976, but the company investors own today was built on the iPhone, launched in 2007, and on the App Store, iCloud and other services sold to the people who own one. Over the past decade it became the world's most profitable hardware maker by charging more for its devices than rivals and then charging their owners again for software and subscriptions.

Two things changed this year. On 20 April Apple named hardware chief John Ternus to succeed Tim Cook, who became executive chairman when the handover took effect on 1 September. And as other tech giants poured cash into AI data centers, Apple, which spends little on them, became what Bloomberg on 22 October called investors' favorite AI hedge and CNBC on 26 October called an "anti-AI trade."

The stock set a record close of **$348.91** on Thursday 29 October, as most of the Magnificent Seven fell. Apple reports at 4:30 p.m. ET on Monday, Ternus's first results call as chief executive.

**Is Apple's business good enough to justify being the market's safe place, at the highest price it has carried in years?**

## The business in one line

Hundreds of millions of people pay Apple every few years for a device and every month for services such as iCloud, Apple Music and App Store purchases, and Google pays it every year to be the default search engine on those devices.

In fiscal 2025 quarters, revenue grew between 4% and 10% from a year earlier; in the first three quarters of fiscal 2026 it grew **15.6%, 16.6% and 16.4%**, by our analysis of Apple's filings.

*Verdict: a phone company with a subscription business attached, and the subscription business earns most of the extra profit.*

## The numbers that matter

### Growth: faster now than its five-year pace

Revenue grew **8.7% a year** over the five fiscal years to September 2025, above the 6.2% median of the 31 hardware makers we track. Over the past two years it grew 10.0% a year, below that group's 15.3%, which is inflated by memory and storage makers riding the AI shortage.

### Profitability: a third of every sale

Apple's operating margin was **33.2%** in the 12 months to June, the share of each sale left after running the business. The median hardware maker keeps 16.9%; Dell keeps 9.4%. Apple's margin is 7.8 percentage points higher than five years ago.

### Cash: $136.7 billion in a year

Free cash flow, the cash left after investment, was **$136.7 billion** in the 12 months to June, 29.3% of sales, against a peer median of 13.5%. Capital spending was under $10 billion over that year, a small fraction of what Microsoft, Alphabet or Meta now spend each quarter.

### Returns: about 80% on capital

By our approximation, Apple earns about **80%** a year on the money tied up in the business, the 95th percentile among hardware makers. It has bought back so much stock that its share count fell 4.1% in two years, which is why earnings per share grew 17.9% a year over five years while revenue grew 8.7%.

### Balance sheet: modest net debt

Apple held $39.5 billion of cash and $84.3 billion of debt on 27 June, **net debt of $44.8 billion**, about a third of a year's operating profit.

*Verdict: among the highest margins, cash conversion and returns of any large company, with the growth rate the one number that is ordinary.*

## Why the stock is where it is

1. **It is the AI hedge.** Apple doesn't need to build data centers to sell its products. In January it struck a multiyear deal to base its AI models on Google's Gemini, CNBC and Yahoo Finance reported, renting rather than building. As the AI trade cracked in October, Apple rose while the rest of the group fell: CNBC noted on 28 October that it and Nvidia were the only Magnificent Seven stocks beating the S&P 500 this year.
2. **The iPhone 18 started well.** iPhone 18 Pro sales in China rose 12% from a year earlier in launch week, in a market that shrank 17%, Counterpoint data reported by Yahoo Finance and Investing.com showed.
3. **But memory costs bite.** On 30 July Apple forecast 9% to 11% growth for the September quarter, short of the roughly 12% Wall Street wanted, and Cook warned of higher memory costs. The stock fell **7.4%** on 31 July.
4. **Some analysts think the iPhone gains won't last.** Loop Capital's Ananda Baruah cut Apple to hold from buy on 20 October, arguing that iPhone shipments cut in the second half of 2026 won't be made up, MacRumors and MacDailyNews reported.

## Testing the worry

**Is the growth borrowed?** Partly. The June quarter's 50.1% gross margin included about 2 percentage points from tariff refunds, and Apple guided to 47% to 48% for September, including about 1 point of refunds, its release and call showed. Those refunds don't recur. Revenue growth itself didn't depend on them: iPhone sales rose about 22% in the June quarter to $54.25 billion.

**Are memory costs temporary?** For now, they're spreading. Jefferies analyst Edison Lee wrote that iPhone gross margin could fall to 34.5% from 38% as memory prices rise. This one is industry-wide: on 29 October Intel blamed the same shortage for weak PC demand. Apple's answer has been price, not volume: the foldable iPhone Duo starts at $1,999.

**Is the AI gap a weakness?** Ternus told WIRED last week that Apple won't "ship AI for AI's sake," Yahoo Finance and The Next Web reported. Renting Gemini keeps Apple's capital spending low; the risk is that it makes Apple a reseller of someone else's model. We think that worry is real but slow-moving: phone owners switch rarely, and the Gemini deal lets Apple upgrade Siri without building the data centers.

*Verdict: the memory squeeze is the worry that survives, and it's a margin problem rather than a demand problem.*

## The moat

- **The installed base.** Owners keep buying, and Services revenue grows faster than device sales because it rides on every phone ever sold, not this year's sales. Services grew 13.5% in fiscal 2025 while iPhone grew 4.2%.
- **Pricing power.** Apple's 47.7% two-year gross margin beats the hardware median of 43.2% while selling far more units than any peer in our data.
- **Scale in chip design.** Apple designs its own processors, which lets it time launches and costs in a way Dell (9.4% operating margin) or HP Enterprise (6.2%) cannot.

Will this business still be here, and stronger, in ten years? We think yes; the open question is how much of Services' profit the courts let it keep.

## The risks we'd watch

**1. Google's payments.** Google paid Apple about **$20 billion** in 2022 to be Safari's default search engine, according to court testimony reported by Macworld and MacRumors; no newer figure has been disclosed. Judge Amit Mehta's September 2025 ruling let the payments continue without exclusivity, Yahoo Finance reported, but the Justice Department has cross-appealed seeking a ban, both companies' quarterly filings show.

The worst case: a ban that ended the payments with no offset. On our arithmetic, $20 billion of nearly pure profit is about 13% of Apple's $155 billion of operating profit over the past 12 months. Offsets: Google may still pay for prominence without exclusivity, and Apple could sell the slot to others. The appeal hasn't been decided.

**2. Memory costs.** If iPhone gross margin fell 3.5 points, as Jefferies suggests, on fiscal 2025's $209.6 billion of iPhone sales, gross profit would drop about $7.3 billion, roughly 5% of operating profit.

**3. The App Store.** The Supreme Court agreed on 30 June to hear Apple's appeal of a contempt finding in Epic Games' case, Yahoo Finance and Investing.com reported. A loss would leave developers freer to route payments around Apple's commission.

The two biggest risks together could take a little under a fifth of operating profit in a bad case, and neither is likely to land all at once.

At 37.4 times expected earnings, Apple needs about four years of 10% annual earnings growth, with the share price flat, for the multiple to fall to about 25 times. Its earnings per share grew 6.9% a year over the three fiscal years to 2025, and 17% in the past 12 months, helped by tariff refunds and buybacks.

Wall Street expects adjusted EPS of $1.98 on Monday, up from $1.85 a year ago, on revenue of $112.95 billion, according to Zacks data reported by Yahoo Finance and TradingView. That's in the upper half of Apple's own range.

## Our verdict

**Business: Strong. Price: Above its usual price.** Apple combines a 33% operating margin, about 80% returns on capital and $137 billion of yearly free cash flow, and its growth has stepped up to the mid-teens. The price already assumes that growth holds: at 39.5 times trailing earnings it sits above its 35.5x median of the past two years. What would change our mind on the business is a ruling ending Google's search payments; what would change our mind on the price is growth sliding back to single digits once tariff refunds fade. The first test comes at 4:30 p.m. ET on Monday, with the guide for the holiday quarter.

Apple is the market's safe place this year, and a safe place priced at 37 times expected earnings has little room for an ordinary quarter.
