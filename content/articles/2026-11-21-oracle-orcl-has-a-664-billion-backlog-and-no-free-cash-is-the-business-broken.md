## How it got here

Oracle built its fortune on the database: since the late 1970s, banks, airlines and governments have stored their records in its software and paid it yearly support fees to keep them running. That base let it buy its way into business applications and, more slowly, into cloud computing, where it trailed Amazon, Microsoft and Google for a decade.

AI changed its position. Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI), its computing-rental business, won giant contracts to host model training, most of all for OpenAI, and its remaining performance obligations, the value of signed contracts not yet delivered, reached **$664 billion** at the end of August, up $209 billion in a year, according to its fiscal first-quarter results reported by Bitget and TenK Digest.

This autumn the bill arrived. Oracle sold $20 billion of stock through the quarter to August, then [raised another $20 billion](/news/2026-11-03/oracle-orcl-sells-its-new-shares-at-116-raising-the-full-20-billion/) in stock and bonds on 2 November, days after an investor day that promised to cap new money. On Friday, as [the Nasdaq fell 5.3%](/news/2026-11-20/stock-market-today-the-s-p-500-enters-a-correction-as-nvidia-sinks-14/), the stock closed at a 52-week low, and Oracle said after the bell that its [OpenAI data centers are fully funded](/news/2026-11-20/oracle-orcl-says-its-openai-data-center-deal-is-fully-funded-after-an-8-drop/). **Can a database company turn a cash-burning AI construction program into a business that pays its owners again?**

## The business in one line

Companies and governments pay Oracle every year to use and support its database and applications, and AI labs pay it, under multi-year contracts, to rent computing power in data centers Oracle builds and equips.

## The numbers that matter

### Growth
Revenue rose **29.6%** in the quarter to August from a year earlier, above the 20.5% median for the 32 software companies we cover. The longer record is weaker: 10.7% a year over five fiscal years, in their bottom quarter. Oracle has guided to at least $90 billion of revenue this fiscal year, up from $67.4 billion, TenK Digest and Bitget reported.

### Profitability
The operating margin over the past 12 months was **32.1%**, in the top quarter of software peers, whose median is 9.7%. But it has fallen 7 points over five years, the steepest drop in the group, as lower-margin computing rentals grow faster than software.

### Cash
This is the problem. Free cash flow, the cash left after spending on data centers, was **negative $28.7 billion** over the 12 months to August, or minus 40% of revenue, the worst of the software companies we cover. Oracle expects capital spending of $90 billion to $95 billion this fiscal year, with no more than $70 billion net of customer prepayments, according to its call as reported by Bitget and TradingKey.

### Returns
Return on equity was 28.3%, in the top quarter of its peers, but that is flattered by debt: equity of $66.8 billion is small next to the money borrowed to build.

### Balance sheet
Oracle carries about **$125 billion** of debt, ERP Today and TIKR reported, against $36.4 billion of cash at the end of August, from its 10-Q. S&P cut its credit rating to BBB- on 9 July, one notch above junk, naming OpenAI as a central risk, MLQ and TechCEO Daily reported. The diluted share count is up 5.2% in two years, against 0.3% for the median software company.

## Why the stock is where it is

1. **It spends far more than it earns.** Negative free cash flow of $28.7 billion in a year means the build-out is funded by lenders and new shareholders, not by the business.
2. **The owners are being diluted.** It sold $20 billion of stock in the quarter to August and $10 billion more at $116 a share on 2 November, alongside $10 billion of bonds, Reuters and RTTNews reported, using the whole new-money allowance through fiscal 2030 it set five days earlier.
3. **One customer carries the order book.** About half of the backlog is tied to OpenAI, S&P estimated when it cut the rating, and Microsoft is [winding down a 2023 deal](/news/2026-11-06/microsoft-msft-is-winding-down-a-deal-to-rent-ai-capacity-from-oracle-orcl/) to rent capacity from Oracle, Bloomberg and Reuters reported on 6 November.
4. **The AI trade turned.** Investors have sold the companies most dependent on AI spending this month; Oracle is down 19.4% in a month by our data.

## Testing the worry

**The cash burn is meant to be temporary, and the date is checkable.** At its 28 October investor day, Oracle said free cash flow turns positive in the second half of fiscal 2028 (from December 2027) and tops $30 billion in fiscal 2029, Yahoo Finance and investingLive reported. Customers are already putting up money in advance: prepayments cut its net spending in the quarter to August to about $18 billion of the $28.5 billion it spent, Bitget and Beancount reported. The worry is contained until fiscal 2028, and then it isn't.

**The dilution is capped, but the cap is used up.** Oracle said it would raise no more than $20 billion of new money from markets from fiscal 2027 to 2030, and it raised exactly that on 2 November. From here, any shortfall must come from prepayments, leases or a broken promise. That makes the next three quarters of cash flow more important, not less.

**The concentration is lasting.** No result this year shrinks OpenAI's share of the backlog quickly, and Microsoft's step back shows big customers can review contracts. Oracle's Friday statement that the OpenAI build is fully funded removes a financing worry, not a customer one. **This is the worry that survives.**

## The moat

1. **The database installed base.** Customers rarely move their core records off Oracle's database, which is why the company can still post a 32.1% operating margin while building data centers at a loss on cash.
2. **Contracted demand.** A $664 billion backlog is more than seven times this year's revenue guide; few companies have this much revenue signed before it is built.
3. **Speed in AI capacity.** OCI grew 121% in the latest quarter, faster than its rivals, though from the smallest base:

| Cloud | Latest quarterly growth | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Oracle OCI | 121% ($7.4 billion) | Results, quarter to August |
| Google Cloud | 103% | [Alphabet breakdown](/news/2026-11-07/alphabet-googl-had-its-worst-day-since-2004-is-the-business-broken/) |
| Microsoft Azure | 48% | [Microsoft recap](/news/2026-10-28/microsoft-msft-beats-q1-estimates-as-azure-growth-speeds-up-to-48/) |
| Amazon AWS | 39% | [Amazon recap](/news/2026-10-29/amazon-amzn-q3-aws-speeds-up-but-ads-and-profit-miss-estimates/) |

The rivals have what Oracle lacks: Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet ($GOOGL) pay for their data centers out of advertising, software and retail profits, not from bond markets.

## The risks we'd watch

1. **OpenAI.** If S&P's half applies to the latest backlog, roughly $330 billion of Oracle's future revenue depends on one company that is itself raising money to pay its bills.
2. **Interest costs.** On about $125 billion of debt, each extra percentage point of interest costs about $1.25 billion a year before tax, about 5% of Oracle's $23.1 billion of operating income over the past 12 months.
3. **More shares.** If the cash turn slips a year, another $10 billion of stock at Friday's price would be about 99 million shares, 3.3% of the 3.02 billion outstanding.

The trailing P/E is at the bottom of its two-year range. Measured against Oracle's own fiscal 2030 target of at least $21 a share in adjusted earnings, set at its investor day, the stock trades at under five times, so the market is giving the plan very little credit.

## Our verdict

**Business: Mixed. Price: Below its usual price.** The software franchise is real and still very profitable, and the backlog is more than seven times this year's expected revenue. But a business with free cash flow at minus 40% of revenue, a rating one notch above junk and half its order book with one customer has not yet shown it can pay its owners. What would change our mind is cash: a visibly smaller burn in its fiscal second-quarter results, expected in mid-December (Oracle hasn't set the date), and a firm path to the fiscal 2028 turn. Oracle is cheap on earnings and expensive on cash, and the cash will decide it.
