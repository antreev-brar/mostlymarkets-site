The story investors are telling about business software fits in one line: AI agents will do the work that companies now pay for one employee login at a time, so they will buy fewer logins, or seats. RBC Capital Markets software analyst Rishi Jaluria put it at its starkest on the RiskReversal podcast, Benzinga and TradingKey reported in their coverage of Tuesday's sell-off.

The peg is this week. On Tuesday, 17 November, Salesforce ($CRM) **sank 9.45%** to $194.52 with no company news, its worst day since 30 May 2024 in our price data, and ServiceNow ($NOW) and Intuit ($INTU) lost 6.5% and 7.2%. Tonight Anthropic is due to price an initial public offering of up to $64.4 billion, and its shares are due to start trading on Thursday. Salesforce is down 26.6% this year, ServiceNow 39.4% and Intuit 51.6%, while Microsoft ($MSFT) is up 4.7%.

## The story in one sentence

> "The AI companies are going to try to destroy Salesforce entirely." Rishi Jaluria, RBC Capital Markets, on the RiskReversal podcast, as quoted by Benzinga on 17 November

The chain runs like this: if agents from Anthropic, OpenAI and the software makers themselves handle customer-service tickets, IT requests and bookkeeping, companies need fewer staff in those jobs. Fewer staff means fewer seats, and Salesforce, ServiceNow, Workday ($WDAY), Atlassian ($TEAM) and Adobe ($ADBE) charge mainly by the seat. Unless they can charge for the agents' work instead, their revenue shrinks with their customers' payrolls.

## The evidence for it

### 1. The sell-off has no company news behind it

Tuesday's drop came with no results, guidance or downgrades from Salesforce, ServiceNow or Intuit ([our story](/news/2026-11-17/salesforce-mongodb-and-sap-shares-slide-what-happened/)). Benzinga and TradingKey tied it to the seat worry and to fund managers raising cash for Anthropic's offering. A fall that big on no news says investors are repricing the whole model, not one quarter.

### 2. Fund managers just made their biggest tech exit since 2006

Bank of America's November survey of fund managers, published on Tuesday, showed the share overweight technology fell to a net 12% from 37% in October, the **biggest one-month cut since 2006**, Bloomberg and CNBC reported. The selling has landed hardest on software: on Tuesday morning the industry was falling about three times as far as tech overall in our data.

### 3. The forecasts have started to slow

ServiceNow had its worst day on record on 29 October after guiding growth in cRPO, the contracted revenue it expects to book over the next 12 months, to **18.5%** from 21% ([our story](/news/2026-10-29/servicenow-now-stock-has-its-worst-day-on-record-after-soft-forecast/)). A week later Atlassian forecast slower subscription growth and sank 13.5% before the open ([our recap](/news/2026-11-06/atlassian-team-beats-q1-estimates-but-sinks-as-it-forecasts-slower-subscription-growth/)). Both companies still grew revenue by more than 20%, but slower forecasts are the first thing the bears said would show up.

### 4. The company building the agents is going public now

Anthropic set a range of $70 to $78 a share for 825 million shares, valuing it at up to about $1.4 trillion fully diluted, The Next Web and Decrypt reported, on top of a $26 billion private placement. On 10 November it launched tools for office work that hit ServiceNow's shares, Fortune and Benzinga reported. That puts a listed rival, and a $64 billion call on investors' cash, next to every software stock this week.

## The case against it

The loudest doubter is Wedbush's Dan Ives, who called the software sell-off earlier this year "a generational opportunity" to own the stalwarts, Fortune and 24/7 Wall St. reported in February. He argued that the big platforms will build AI in rather than be replaced. Three points hold up to the same standard as the bear case:

1. **Revenue is still growing.** In the latest quarter in our SEC data, sales grew 10.8% at Salesforce, 20.5% at ServiceNow, 13.7% at Intuit, 12.9% at Adobe and 12.8% at Workday from a year earlier. None has yet reported a fall in seats that shows up in revenue.
2. **The incumbents are selling agents too.** Salesforce said on 26 August that annual recurring revenue from Agentforce, its AI agent product, topped $1.5 billion, up more than 240%, though the measure now includes other AI products. ServiceNow raised its 2026 target for AI contract value to $1.7 billion on 28 October, its release and Subscription Insider reported.
3. **The prices already assume a decline.** Salesforce and Intuit trade at about 13 times forward earnings and Adobe at 8.8 times, against a 25.6 times median for the software companies we track. Each earns 34% to 41% of its sales as free cash flow, a top-quarter figure among peers.

## What would change minds

- **Anthropic's debut, Thursday 19 November.** Pricing above the $70 to $78 range and a strong first day would confirm where investors think AI's profits will land, and keep pulling money from software.
- **Intuit's results, after the close Thursday.** Intuit's tax and bookkeeping software is the kind of work agents target; a cut to its full-year forecast would be the first crack in revenue, not just in forecasts.
- **Workday and Autodesk on Tuesday 24 November, then Salesforce on Wednesday 2 December.** Salesforce guided third-quarter cRPO growth to about 14%; a miss, or a warning about customers cutting seats from Workday, would turn the story into evidence.

## Our read

**Partly supported.** The selling is real and the forecasts at ServiceNow and Atlassian have started to slow, but no big seat seller has yet reported shrinking revenue, and their prices already assume a lot of damage: Salesforce trades at half its two-year median multiple. The weakest link is the jump from "agents can do this work" to "customers are cutting seats now", which the reported numbers don't show yet. The one number we'll watch is Salesforce's cRPO growth on 2 December against its own guide of about 14%.
