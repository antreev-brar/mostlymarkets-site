## How it got here

Visa began as Bank of America's credit card program in 1958 and became a public company in 2008, owned until then by the banks that issued its cards. It doesn't lend money or issue cards. It runs the network that carries a payment from a shopper's bank to a merchant's bank, and takes a small fee for each one.

That model has compounded quietly. Revenue grew from $18.4 billion in fiscal 2017 to **$45.9 billion** in fiscal 2026, which ended in September, according to its SEC filings, and $1,000 in the stock five years ago was worth $1,982 on Monday.

This year tested it three times. On 13 January President Trump endorsed a bill to open card routing to rival networks and Visa fell 4.5%; on 23 February a stablecoin research report knocked off another 4.5%; and by 27 March the stock had closed at $295.52, down 15.7% for the year. Since then it has risen 44%. **Were the threats overdone, or is the market now ignoring them?**

## The business in one line

Banks pay Visa a fee on each card payment and each dollar spent, every time a card is used, plus extra for cross-border payments and services such as fraud scoring.

Revenue fell only in fiscal 2020, when the pandemic stopped travel, and has grown faster in the last five years (13.7% a year) than over the full nine (10.7%). Cross-border payments are the most profitable line and the most cyclical one. *Verdict: a toll road on spending that grows with prices, travel and the shift from cash.*

## The numbers that matter

### Growth
Revenue grew **13.7% a year** over five fiscal years, against a 9.4% median for the 46 payment firms, exchanges and asset managers in our data, and 13.2% in the latest quarter. Mastercard ($MA) grew a little faster, 16.5% a year. *Above its peers, slightly behind its closest rival.*

### Profitability
Visa kept **61.2%** of revenue as operating profit in the 12 months to September, in the top tenth of its peers (median 26.8%). That is 4.3 points lower than five years ago, but the drop is mostly lawsuits: Visa booked $1.7 billion of litigation provisions as special items in fiscal 2026, according to its results release. Add them back and the margin is about **65%**, in line with the 64% to 66% it earned from fiscal 2017 to 2024. *The core margin hasn't slipped.*

### Cash
Free cash flow was **$22.1 billion**, or 48.1% of revenue, in the 12 months to September, against a 24.5% median for its peers. Capital spending was about $1.7 billion, under 4% of revenue. *Almost everything it earns is free to hand back.*

### Returns
Visa earned about **49%** on the capital tied up in it, against a 10.6% peer median, and 65% on its equity. Mastercard's return on equity is higher, at 251%, mostly because buybacks have shrunk its book equity to $6.8 billion. *Returns show a business that needs almost no money to grow.*

### Balance sheet
Visa had $12.9 billion of cash against $22.1 billion of debt at the end of September, less than one year of free cash flow in net debt. In April the board approved a new $20 billion buyback program, BNN Bloomberg and Rallies reported. *Conservative, with room to keep buying back stock.*

## Why the stock is where it is

From a worried investor's side, four forces drove the stock this year:

1. **Washington.** On 13 January Trump endorsed the Credit Card Competition Act, which would make large banks offer merchants a second network on credit cards, one that isn't Visa or Mastercard. Visa fell 4.5% that day, the WSJ and Bloomberg reported.
2. **Stablecoins and AI agents.** A Citrini Research report on Sunday 22 February argued that AI shopping agents could move payments onto stablecoin rails and around card fees, Seeking Alpha and GuruFocus reported; Visa fell 4.5% the next day.
3. **Merchant lawsuits.** The 21-year US case over swipe fees is heading to a $38 billion settlement shared with Mastercard, and more than 1,000 merchants, including Walmart, object ([our brief](/news/2026-11-17/visa-v-and-mastercard-ask-a-judge-to-approve-their-38-billion-swipe-fee-deal/)).
4. **Slower growth ahead.** Visa guided fiscal 2027 net revenue growth to the low double digits, below this year's 15% ([our recap](/news/2026-10-27/visa-v-beats-q4-estimates-but-its-2027-outlook-points-to-slower-growth/)).

The turn came on results. Visa rose 8.3% on 29 April after a fiscal second-quarter beat, a raised forecast and the new buyback, BNN Bloomberg and TIKR reported, and closed at its highest since at least 2021 on 4 November.

## Testing the worry

**Washington: lasting, but stalled.** The bill would not cap Visa's fees; it would let banks route some credit payments to a rival. Goldman Sachs analyst Will Nance estimated in January that a 5% loss of credit card volume would cut Visa's earnings by about 3%, and called the hit "manageable", Benzinga and Finviz reported. The bill has not passed the Senate: sponsors failed to attach it to a housing bill this year, Payments Dive reported, and it was still pending at the end of September, the Washington Times reported. *A real risk with a measured cost, and not law.*

**Stablecoins: temporary as a stock worry, lasting as a trend.** Visa's stablecoin settlement runs at more than **$25 billion a year**, ten times a year ago, Chief Executive Ryan McInerney said on the October call, according to Investing.com's transcript and FinanceFeeds. Against payments volume of about $4.1 trillion in the September quarter alone, as Digital Transactions and TIKR reported, that is under 0.2% of a year's volume, and it runs through Visa, not around it. *So far stablecoins are a product Visa sells, not a rival taking volume.*

**Lawsuits: contained, and mostly paid for.** Visa has been reserving for this case for years. In fiscal 2026 it recorded $1.6 billion of new accruals for the US case and put $1.3 billion into its litigation escrow account, according to its 10-K. The settlement's rate cuts apply to interchange, the fee that goes to the banks that issue cards, not to Visa's own network fees. *A cost already in the numbers.*

**Slower growth: the worry that survives.** Client incentives grew 21% in the fourth quarter, faster than gross revenue, which means Visa pays banks more each year to keep their cards on its network. Guidance for low-double-digit revenue growth in fiscal 2027 is the first slowdown since the post-pandemic rebound. *This is the one that matters for the price.*

## The moat

- **A two-sided network.** Merchants accept Visa because shoppers carry it, and banks issue it because merchants accept it. Visa processed 74.2 billion transactions in the September quarter, up 10%, according to its release.
- **Pricing power.** Its 10-K lists "select pricing modifications" among the drivers of both service and data processing revenue in fiscal 2026: Visa raised prices and volume still grew.
- **Almost no capital needs.** Capital spending was under 4% of revenue, so growth costs Visa almost nothing.

## The risks we'd watch

1. **The card bill becomes law.** Our arithmetic on Goldman's estimate: if twice the volume left, 10% of credit, Visa's earnings would fall by about 6%, roughly half a year of the earnings growth it guides to. The offsets are that debit and cross-border payments are outside the bill, and that Goldman assumed a take rate it said may overstate the hit. In proportion: a bad year, not a broken business.
2. **The Justice Department's debit case.** The government's 2024 suit says Visa illegally monopolized US debit; a judge refused to dismiss it in June 2025, and a magistrate moved a key deadline to 29 January 2027, Payments Dive and CU Today reported. A loss could force changes to how Visa prices debit, which is not yet sizeable.
3. **Incentives outrun revenue.** If client incentives keep growing about 6 points faster than gross revenue, as in the fourth quarter (21% against about 15%), net revenue grows about 2 points a year slower than the business underneath it, because incentives equal about 30% of gross revenue.

## What the price assumes

| Measure | Now | Its history | Peers |
|---|---|---|---|
| P/E, expected earnings | 29.0x | 26.6x five-year average (TIKR) | 17.1x median; Mastercard 30.5x |
| P/E, trailing (GAAP) | 34.1x | About 29x to 31x five-year average (Alpha Spread, FullRatio, WiseSheets) | Mastercard 33.9x |
| Price to free cash flow | 35.3x | n/a | Mastercard 35.1x |

At 29 times expected earnings, Visa trades at about the same multiple as Mastercard and 70% above the median of its broader peer group. If earnings grow 12% a year, the top of Visa's guidance, for five years, today's price would be about **16.5 times** the 2031 figure, about the peer median now. The free cash flow yield of 2.8% is below the 10-year Treasury's 5.1%, so the price leans on that growth.

## Our verdict

**Business: Strong. Price: Above its usual price.** Visa is a toll on spending with margins near 65% before lawsuits, capital needs under 4% of revenue and five years of 13.7% annual growth, and this year's three threats each turned out smaller or slower than the selloffs priced. The price is the catch: at 29 times expected earnings against a five-year average of about 26.6 times, it assumes the growth guidance holds while incentives keep rising. Passage of the card bill, or net revenue growth falling below 10% in the coming quarters, would change our mind. The next tests are Judge Brian Cogan's ruling on the settlement and Visa's fiscal first-quarter results in late January. A great business, priced as if nothing will touch its fee.
