Exelon ($EXC), which owns the power lines that serve Chicago, Philadelphia, Baltimore and Washington, reported third-quarter adjusted operating earnings of 87 cents a share on Friday, 30 October, up from 86 cents a year earlier and above the 78 cents analysts expected, according to TheFly; the Nasdaq consensus was 80 cents. On a GAAP basis, which counts one-time items, EPS fell to 70 cents from 86 cents, the release showed. The stock **edged up 0.5%** to $46.16 on Friday, a day the S&P 500 fell 1.8%.

## What stood out

The beat came against a low bar. Analysts' 78-cent estimate was below the 86 cents Exelon earned a year earlier, so the quarter's 1-cent gain cleared it by **9 cents**. The gap between adjusted and GAAP earnings, 17 cents a share, is the one-time items the adjusted figure leaves out, the release showed.

Data centers are the long-run story. Management said on the call that it has identified about 11 gigawatts of high-probability new load through 2034, with about 10% of it expected to come online by 2028, according to the transcripts. Before the report, Tikr noted Exelon had cut its broader data-center pipeline to 36 gigawatts from 43 after a stricter review, while keeping its $41 billion capital plan through 2029.

Exelon only delivers power, so it earns a regulated return on the wires and substations it builds. More data centers mean more of that building, which is why the size of the high-probability pipeline matters more than any one quarter.

## The outlook

Exelon raised the bottom of its 2026 forecast to $2.85 a share, from $2.81, keeping the top at $2.91, according to its investor presentation, and management said it aims to deliver at the midpoint or better.

The stock is up 5.9% this year after rising 14.3% in October, by our price files, and sits 8.2% below its 52-week closing high of $50.29 from 16 March. At 15.7 times expected earnings, it is cheaper than the 18.6 median for the utilities we cover.

## Our take

A solid but unspectacular quarter: the beat reflects a cautious estimate more than a surge in profit, and the narrower range nudges the midpoint up by 2 cents. The number to watch is the high-probability data-center load: if more of the 36-gigawatt pipeline firms into signed contracts, the $41 billion spending plan has room to grow.
