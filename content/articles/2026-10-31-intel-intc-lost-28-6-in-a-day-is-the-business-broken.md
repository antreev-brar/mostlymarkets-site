## How it got here

Intel ($INTC), founded in 1968, designs the processors inside PCs and servers and makes chips in its own factories, which it reports as a separate business, Intel Foundry. Its sales peaked at **$79.0 billion** in 2021, according to its filings.

Sales then shrank four years in a row, to $52.9 billion in 2025. Intel lost $18.8 billion in 2024, and its free cash flow was negative every year from 2022 to 2025.

In August 2025 the US government took a stake of about 10% and became Intel's largest shareholder, Reuters reported.

The turn came in 2026. AI is moving from training models to running them and to software "agents" that act on their own, and that shift "is significantly increasing the need for Intel's CPUs," Chief Executive Lip-Bu Tan said in the first-quarter results release on 23 April. Data-center sales growth sped up from 22% in the first quarter to 78% in the third, according to Intel's releases.

The stock **jumped 23.6%** on 24 April, the day after that report, and closed at a peak of $140.94 on 22 June.

Then came Thursday's fourth-quarter forecast, well short of estimates ([our recap](/news/2026-10-29/intel-intc-q3-profit-tops-estimates-shares-plunge-on-a-weak-forecast/)). On Friday the stock **plunged 28.6%**, its worst day since 1974, CNN reported. **Is the business broken, or did the price run too far ahead of it?**

## The business in one line

PC makers and data-center operators buy Intel's processors chip by chip, in orders that rise and fall with PC sales and server spending, while Intel Foundry makes chips almost entirely for Intel itself.

## How it makes money

Two units bring in almost all the money, and in the third quarter they went in opposite directions, according to Intel's results release.

| Segment | What it sells | Share of Q3 sales | Growth in Q3, year on year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Client Computing and Physical AI | Processors for laptops and desktops | 49% ($8.1 billion) | -5% |
| Data Center and AI | Xeon processors for servers | 44% ($7.3 billion) | +78% |

Revenue sat near **$13 billion a quarter** for two years before jumping in the second quarter of 2026.

## The numbers that matter

### Growth: up 12%, a third of AMD's pace

Revenue rose **12.1%** to $59.9 billion in the 12 months to September 2026.

AMD ($AMD) grew 39.5% and Nvidia ($NVDA) 83.4% over their latest 12 months, according to their filings.

### Profitability: better, from a low base

Intel kept **15.3%** of its third-quarter revenue as operating profit, up from 5.0% a year earlier, according to its filings.

Over the past 12 months its gross margin, the share of sales left after making the chips, was 39.9%, against AMD's 53.2%.

It's falling again: Intel expects its adjusted gross margin to drop to 38.0% in the fourth quarter, from 44.1% in the third.

Profit falls faster than sales: at the forecast's midpoint, revenue slips about 6% from the third quarter, while adjusted earnings per share fall by more than half, to 25 cents. A company that owns its factories pays for them whether or not they run full, so a small dip in sales cuts deep into profit.

### Where the profit is: servers earn it, the factories lose it

The server unit earned **$3.04 billion** of operating profit in the third quarter, more than 40 cents on each dollar of sales, according to the release.

The PC unit earned $2.29 billion, and Intel Foundry lost $1.85 billion.

A year earlier the PC unit earned almost three times as much as the server unit; servers overtook it in the second quarter of 2026, according to Intel's releases.

### Cash: thin, and spending is rising

Free cash flow, the cash left after spending on plants and equipment, was **$3.8 billion** in the 12 months to September, 6.3% of sales and less than a third of AMD's rate.

Gross capital spending, which Intel is raising to make more chips, was $4.2 billion in the third quarter, up 43% from a year earlier, by our calculation from the release.

### Balance sheet: a little more cash than debt

Intel ended September with $54.0 billion of cash and short-term investments against **$50.1 billion** of debt, according to the release. The balance sheet isn't the problem.

## Why the stock is where it is

1. **The forecast went backwards.** Intel guided fourth-quarter revenue to $15.0 billion to $16.0 billion, below the $17.07 billion analysts expected, according to LSEG data cited by Reuters. Even the top of that range is below what Intel sold in the third quarter.
2. **PCs are shrinking because AI is taking the memory.** On the call, Tan pointed to "the unprecedented memory crunch and the resulting softness in PC demand," NBC News reported. Chief Financial Officer David Zinsner said in the release that he sees the PC slowdown "continuing at least through the first half of next year."
3. **Intel can't make enough of what's selling.** "I'm disappointed that we were not able to meet strong customer demand in this quarter," Tan said on the call, according to NBC. Jay Goldberg, an analyst at Seaport Research Partners, said Intel is "producing too many chips for consumer PCs in the worst PC market in 15 years" and not enough for AI servers, according to Reuters: "Bad planning is the cause."
4. **An accident at a factory.** A deadly explosion in October at Intel's plant in Kiryat Gat, Israel, will cut about $300 million from fourth-quarter revenue, Reuters reported.
5. **The price had run far ahead.** At its June peak, Intel was valued at about 13 times its sales, by our calculation from its filings and our prices, against a five-year median of 2.4. Morgan Stanley analysts, who cut their price target, pointed to "how expensive the stock is on next-year numbers," Investopedia reported.

## Testing the worry

**The accident is temporary, and small.** It accounts for about $300 million, less than a fifth of the $1.6 billion gap between the midpoint of Intel's forecast and what analysts expected.

**The PC slump is lasting, and it's spreading.** SK hynix expects memory demand to outrun supply through 2030, and TrendForce forecast on 30 September that contract prices for ordinary DRAM would rise another 10% to 15% this quarter, with higher component costs in notebook inventories weighing on sales.

Dell ($DELL) fell 10.8% and HP ($HPQ) 9.6% on Friday, and Samsung's phone business swung to an operating loss from a profit a year earlier, on higher component costs, memory included, the Korea JoongAng Daily reported.

**The supply shortfall looks temporary, if the spending works.** Zinsner said Intel will keep investing "to increase supply and support future growth." The "bad planning" Goldberg blames can be fixed, but the fix costs cash, and Intel keeps little: its free cash flow over the past year was less than it spent on plants and equipment in the third quarter alone.

**The worry that survives is the price.** The business isn't broken, but even after Friday the stock costs more than twice its usual multiple of sales: the price assumes Intel is already fixed.

## The moat

Intel has three real advantages, and rivals beat it on the numbers that measure them.

- **Scale in PCs.** Its PC unit still brought in $8.1 billion in a bad quarter for PCs and earned 28 cents of operating profit per dollar.
- **Servers in the AI-agent boom.** The third quarter brought Intel's "strongest data center revenue growth in 15 years," Zinsner said in the release.
- **Its own factories, with Washington as its largest shareholder.** Intel Foundry's sales grew 58%, nearly all of them to Intel itself. Its chipmaking rival, Taiwan Semiconductor ($TSM), is worth about $2.1 trillion, six times Intel's market value.

## The risks we'd watch

1. **PCs are half the business.** The PC unit brought in about half of third-quarter revenue, so each further 10% fall in its sales would cost about $0.8 billion a quarter.
2. **The factories keep losing money.** Intel Foundry's third-quarter loss was a fifth smaller than a year earlier, but had it broken even, Intel's operating profit would have been $4.4 billion, not $2.5 billion.
3. **Cash could turn negative again.** Free cash flow was negative $2.5 billion in the first quarter of 2026, and capital spending is rising. Another quarter like that would erase two-thirds of the past year's total.

## What the price assumes

Intel's losses in 2024 and 2025 leave no useful history for its price-to-earnings ratio, so we also measure it against sales and cash flow.

| Measure | Now | Its history | Peers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forward P/E | 38.4 | No useful history: losses in 2024 and 2025 | Nvidia 16.9, Qualcomm 17.8, AMD 44.8 |
| Price to sales | 5.8 | Five-year median 2.4; about 13 at the June peak | Qualcomm 3.9, Nvidia 16.3, AMD 20.0 |
| Price to free cash flow | 92 | Negative free cash flow every year from 2022 to 2025 | Qualcomm 16.6, Nvidia 38.8, AMD 98.1 |

Price to sales and price to free cash flow are our calculations from SEC filings, our price history and Friday's market values.

The price assumes a full recovery in profit. Intel is worth about **$348 billion** at Friday's close.

That's 1.7 times its value at the end of October 2021, when its net income ran at about $20 billion a year, by our calculation from our prices and its filings.

Suppose Intel made 2018's net margin, its best in our records back to 2016, on the past year's sales. That's about $17.8 billion of profit, and the stock would still trade at about 20 times it.

So the price already pays for Intel's best margins in a decade. Nvidia, whose sales grew 83%, trades at 17 times forward earnings.

## Our verdict

**Business: Mixed. Price: Above its usual price.**

The business isn't broken. Its servers grew 78% and now earn more than its PCs, and it holds a little more cash than debt.

But half its sales come from PCs, which Intel expects to stay weak until at least mid-2027, its factories still lose money, and the cash it keeps is thin. At 5.8 times sales against a five-year median of 2.4, the price assumes the turnaround is finished.

What would change our mind is proof that the supply fix works: server growth with the gross margin back above the third quarter's 44.1%, or a big outside customer for Intel Foundry. AMD's report on Tuesday, 3 November, is the next test: steady PC chip sales there would point to Intel losing share, not just a weak market.

A business that's getting better, priced as if it had already gotten there.
